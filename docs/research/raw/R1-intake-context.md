### RQ-S0-1. How do organisations running critical services tier changes by risk before work starts, and what signals feed the tier?

**Answer.** Two independent mechanisms recur: a change-category scheme (ITIL's standard/normal/emergency, decided by a change-approval role using CAB advice) that gates *process weight* — how much assessment and authorization a change needs before it starts — and a service-tier scheme (Google's per-service risk-tolerance tiers, Uber's 0–5 service-criticality tiers) that gates *rollout* pacing once a change ships. Google's SRE book ties risk tolerance to consequence and revenue, decided jointly by the product team and the infrastructure provider, not by a fixed formula. Uber's scheme is used automatically at deploy time: services are pre-classified 0 (most critical) to 5 (least), and a commit's rollout is staged tier-by-tier with error-rate gates between stages, discovered because 1.4% of commits in Uber's Go monorepo affect more than 100 services and 0.3% affect more than 1,000. None of the primary sources found describe a single "assign a risk tier before the ticket starts" workflow that combines schema/data-change detection with historical incident density in one score — that composite is closest to what secondary/vendor blast-radius material (blast radius depends on dependency depth, service criticality, and CMDB freshness) describes, not something confirmed in a primary source.

**Patterns found.**
- ITIL standard / normal / emergency change categories, each with different pre-approval and assessment weight — [ITIL Change Categories](https://blog.invgate.com/what-are-the-itil-change-categories), secondary (vendor practice explainer of the ITIL/AXELOS standard). Standard changes are pre-approved after a first-time risk assessment; normal changes get a full RFC plus risk/impact assessment plus CAB-advised authorization; emergency changes get expedited assessment with a mandatory post-implementation review.
- Change Manager (not the CAB) holds approval authority, using CAB members' advice on risk and impact — [Change Advisory Board Explained](https://advisera.com/20000academy/knowledgebase/change-advisory-board-itil-advise-approve/), secondary practice explainer of ITIL.
- Google ties per-service risk tolerance to consequence of failure and revenue impact, decided by the product team for consumer-facing services and jointly for infrastructure services exposing cost/reliability tiers — [Embracing Risk, Google SRE Book](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/), primary (company practice doc). Quote: "these enterprises depend on Google Apps for Work services... to provide tools that enable their employees to perform their daily work" versus YouTube where "rapid feature development was correspondingly more important."
- Uber's automated deploy orchestrator groups services into criticality tiers 0–5 and stages rollout tier-by-tier with an error-rate kill switch — [Controlling the Rollout of Large-Scale Monorepo Changes, Uber Engineering Blog](https://www.uber.com/en-CL/blog/controlling-the-rollout-of-large-scale-monorepo-changes/), primary (company engineering blog). Measured: from 500,000 analysed commits in Uber's Go monorepo, 1.4% touch >100 services and 0.3% touch >1,000 services; orchestration is designed to complete within 24 hours.
- Blast radius (dependency depth × service criticality × CMDB freshness) as the signal that should be scoped before a change request is approved, with escalation if the team cannot state it — [Which Changes Need a Full Production Readiness Review?](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-08-06-risk-tiered-production-readiness-reviews/view), secondary (vendor practice blog, not a named organisation's disclosed process).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for the Google and Uber patterns (company sources), each independently corroborated by no other primary source for the exact same claim — i.e., single-source per organisation, but the two organisations converge on the same underlying signal (service criticality / consequence of failure) via different mechanisms (design-time risk-tolerance vs. automated rollout tiering). ITIL classification is from secondary explainers of a published standard, not the AXELOS/ITIL primary text itself. The composite "assign risk tier from schema-change + historical-incident-density signals before work starts" is not evidenced in any primary source found; it is inferred from separate signals in separate sources.

**Transfer to an agent.** The pattern transfers as two separate rubric lines, not one: (1) an S0 eligibility check keyed to a pre-declared per-service criticality tier (à la Uber's 0–5) that determines whether the ticket is eligible for the factory at all and how much S3 review weight it gets; (2) within S7, the same tier gates rollout pace and kill-condition sensitivity, following Uber's staged-cohort model. Pipeline rule: at S0, look up the target service's declared criticality tier and the change's declared type (schema/data change, new integration, config-only) from the service catalogue, and route anything above a threshold tier to mandatory human eligibility sign-off before the factory starts.

**Gaps.** Searched for a single named-organisation "risk score at intake" combining schema-change detection with historical incident density; not found in any primary source — only vendor/secondary blog material describes such composites. Searched Meta engineering blog for a published risk-aware deployment tiering scheme; none surfaced in the time budget (only Uber's was found with public numbers). The Google SRE Workbook's dedicated change-management chapter (as opposed to the sre-book "Embracing Risk" chapter) returned only a landing page for a report PDF; the PDF itself was not fetched.

### RQ-S0-2. What "definition of ready" criteria do mature teams require before a ticket may start, and which of those are machine-checkable?

**Answer.** Definition of Ready (DoR) checklists split cleanly into two kinds of criteria: presence checks on fields (acceptance criteria filled in, story points estimated, an epic link set) that a tool can verify automatically, and judgment calls (is the story actually small enough, are dependencies really resolved, does the team understand it) that require a human or an LLM judge, not a field check. Scrum.org and Atlassian both frame DoR as a team-owned, non-mandatory agreement rather than a Scrum Guide requirement, built typically around the INVEST properties (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable). A concrete machine-checkable instantiation exists in tooling: Atlassian's own Definition-of-Ready Jira Marketplace app checks exactly three things — Epic Link is not empty, Story Points is not empty, and Description/Acceptance Criteria is not empty.

**Patterns found.**
- DoR as a non-mandatory, team-defined checklist to guide whether a Product Backlog Item can enter a sprint — [Trying to Keep Things Simple - "Ready", Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/trying-keep-things-simple-ready), primary (practitioner practice doc from the Scrum governing body). The Scrum Guide itself only requires the team be "confident they can deliver the item... within the sprint timebox" — DoR is an optional refinement on top of that.
- INVEST as the underlying completeness frame for what "ready" should test: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable — [Definition of Ready and the INVEST Principle](https://full.cx/blog/definition-of-ready-invest-principles), secondary summary of the INVEST mnemonic (Bill Wake).
- A concrete, field-level machine-checkable DoR: Epic Link not empty, Story Points not empty, Description/Acceptance Criteria not empty — [Definition of Ready, Atlassian Marketplace listing](https://marketplace.atlassian.com/apps/1224751/definition-of-ready), primary (official tool documentation for an Atlassian Marketplace app enforcing DoR as a Jira workflow gate).
- Acceptance criteria are explicitly the field carrying the judgment content ("what success looks like," translated to verifiable requirements) — that content itself is judgment-based to write and evaluate, even though its mere presence is machine-checkable — [What is Acceptance Criteria?, Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/work-management/project-management/acceptance-criteria), primary (official vendor practice doc).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for Scrum.org's own framing (the body that stewards Scrum) and for Atlassian's own tool behaviour; the INVEST synthesis is secondary. Two independent primary sources (Scrum.org, Atlassian) agree that DoR is team-defined rather than standardized, and that at minimum "acceptance criteria present" is the closest thing to a universal criterion. No source gives defect-rate or cycle-time data tying DoR adoption to outcome — this pattern is normative/practitioner consensus, not measured.

**Transfer to an agent.** The presence checks transfer directly as a mechanical S0/S2 gate: reject or flag a ticket if acceptance criteria, an owner, and a linked design/epic are absent — this is a one-line rubric check ("ticket has non-empty acceptance-criteria field, named owner, and linked parent") the pipeline can run without an LLM. The judgment checks (is it actually small enough, is it actually INVEST-testable) do not transfer as mechanical gates; they map to the agent's S2 completeness assessment (RQ-S2 territory) rather than to S0, since S0 in the charter produces nothing and only decides eligibility.

**Gaps.** Searched for INVEST's original source (Bill Wake) directly rather than a secondary summary; not fetched within budget — the description above relies on a secondary explainer, though INVEST's content (six named properties) is stable and uncontested across the sources checked. No published data found on defect or rework rates attributable to DoR adoption specifically (as distinct from acceptance-criteria completeness generally, which is RQ-S2-1's territory).

### RQ-S0-3. What kinds of change do organisations explicitly exclude from automated or AI-assisted flows, and why?

**Answer.** Published, named-organisation policy on this is thin. The one primary, official source found — GitHub's own guardrails documentation for its Copilot cloud agent — draws the exclusion boundary at configuration/secrets and at the merge action itself: agents are hard-blocked from pushing to default branches or merging PRs, and teams are told to protect Copilot/MCP configuration files with CODEOWNERS and keep tokens the agent shouldn't see in Actions secrets rather than in-repo. Beyond that single primary source, the recurring exclusion categories reported across secondary/practitioner material are consistent but not attributable to a single named company's disclosed policy: authentication, payments/billing, infrastructure, data/schema migrations, and anything touching PII or secrets are repeatedly named as requiring mandatory human sign-off rather than autonomous AI action, on the reasoning that these are the areas where an error has direct financial, security, or irreversible-data consequences. The 2025 DORA State of AI-assisted Software Development report frames the risk generically rather than by category: higher AI adoption raises both delivery throughput and delivery instability, and the effect is amplified, not created, by whatever process maturity (version control discipline, automated testing) already exists.

**Patterns found.**
- Hard technical guardrails: Copilot cloud agent is prevented by default from pushing to default branches or merging PRs; CODEOWNERS-protected config files; secrets kept out of agent-visible storage — [Building guardrails for GitHub Copilot cloud agent, GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/tutorials/cloud-agent/build-guardrails), primary (official tool documentation).
- Named exclusion categories recurring across practitioner/vendor sources: auth, payments, secrets, infrastructure, data migrations, public APIs, and PII require explicit human sign-off before merge — [establishing code review standards for AI-generated code](https://www.metacto.com/blogs/establishing-code-review-standards-for-ai-generated-code) and similar, secondary/vendor practitioner blogs, not a disclosed named-company policy.
- AI adoption amplifies existing delivery instability rather than creating a new category of risk on its own; the mitigating factor found is process maturity (version control, automated testing), not a change-type exclusion list — [DORA 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/), primary (industry research programme, Google Cloud/DORA).

**Evidence quality.** One primary official source (GitHub) for hard tool-level guardrails; everything else is secondary/vendor-practitioner content converging on the same short list of sensitive domains without a citable named-company policy document. No financial-services engineering blog with a disclosed AI-coding exclusion policy was found despite four targeted searches (JPMorgan-, bank-, and payments-specific queries all returned marketing or generic vendor content, not primary engineering disclosures). This question's "good answer" bar (named exclusions, reasoning, enforcement mechanism) is only fully met by the GitHub source; the rest is pattern-level consensus, not verified organisational fact.

**Transfer to an agent.** Transfers directly and cleanly: the factory's own anti-goal ("no autonomous merge or deploy") already matches GitHub's hard-guardrail pattern, so the rubric line is to encode the same two mechanisms — a tool-level block on the merge/deploy action regardless of agent judgment, and a CODEOWNERS-equivalent (declared owner or named reviewer) requirement on any path touching auth, payments, secrets, migrations, or infra config, sourced from the service catalogue rather than left to the agent to infer per ticket.

**Gaps.** Searched specifically for Anthropic and OpenAI's own internal-usage disclosures naming excluded code categories (as opposed to general safety/misuse policy); none found describing engineering-change exclusions specifically. Searched for a financial-services engineering blog (bank or payments company) with a disclosed AI-coding-tool exclusion policy; none found — this is a genuine gap, not just a budget limit, since none of eight search results across two queries surfaced a primary disclosure.

### RQ-S1-1. How do large organisations determine which services a change impacts before it ships, and which of those methods work outside a monorepo?

**Answer.** Four distinct, independently-evolved methods exist, each with a different cost/accuracy/monorepo-dependence trade-off: build-graph reverse-dependency queries (Bazel `rdeps()`, used to compute exactly which downstream targets a change affects) are cheap and precise but require a unified build graph, so they are essentially monorepo-only; service catalogues (Backstage) encode declared `dependsOn`/`providesApis` relationships that any team can populate regardless of repo layout, but accuracy is only as good as manual upkeep; and trace-derived dependency graphs (Netflix's Service Topology) observe actual runtime call patterns via eBPF, IPC metrics, and distributed traces, which works across any repo topology because it observes running services rather than source code, at the cost of sampling gaps and no compile-time guarantee. Uber's monorepo-scale finding — that a small fraction of commits (1.4% of 500,000 analysed) touch more than 100 services — is itself only knowable because Uber has both a unified build graph and automated cross-service deploy orchestration; polyrepo teams without an equivalent graph must fall back to the catalogue or trace-derived methods, which are weaker on precision but do not require a shared repo.

**Patterns found.**
- Bazel `rdeps()` reverse-dependency queries compute exactly which build/test targets are affected by a changed target or file, usable as a pre-CI step to scope tests and impact — [A practical guide to Bazel query, with examples, Buildkite](https://buildkite.com/resources/blog/a-guide-to-bazel-query/), secondary (practitioner/vendor guide over Bazel's own semantics); confirmed by [Bazel Query How-To, bazel.googlesource.com](https://bazel.googlesource.com/bazel/+/2fb9caa4f9c6a90bc68db19df694530f589a23e9/site/en/docs/query-how-to.md), primary (official tool documentation). Works precisely because it walks an explicit, unified build graph — a monorepo/single-build-graph precondition.
- Backstage's software catalog models services as entities connected by `dependsOn`/`providesApis` relations, queryable as a dependency/ownership graph independent of repo topology — [Creating the Catalog Graph, Backstage official docs](https://backstage.io/docs/features/software-catalog/creating-the-catalog-graph/), primary (official tool documentation, originated at Spotify). Works in polyrepo settings because entities are declared metadata, not derived from a shared build.
- Uber's monorepo blast-radius measurement: of 500,000 analysed commits to the Go monorepo, 1.4% touch more than 100 services and 0.3% touch more than 1,000 — [Controlling the Rollout of Large-Scale Monorepo Changes, Uber Engineering Blog](https://www.uber.com/en-CL/blog/controlling-the-rollout-of-large-scale-monorepo-changes/), primary (company engineering blog). This measurement itself depends on Uber's unified build/deploy graph.
- Netflix derives a real-time service dependency map from eBPF network flows, IPC metrics, and aggregated distributed traces, combinable or queryable independently, used for blast-radius analysis and production change management — [How Netflix Maps Thousands of Microservices in Real-Time, InfoQ summary of Netflix TechBlog](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/06/netflix-microservices-realtime/), secondary (trade-press summary; the primary Netflix TechBlog post returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch). Explicit limitation noted: eBPF flows lack application-level (API-endpoint) context, and trace-derived views are subject to sampling.

**Evidence quality.** Primary for Bazel semantics and Backstage's data model (official tool docs); primary for Uber's measured commit-impact figures (company blog with a stated denominator); secondary for the Netflix pattern because the primary TechBlog post could not be fetched directly (403) and the finding relies on an InfoQ summary of it — this should be re-verified against the primary post if possible. Three independent organisations (Uber, Spotify/Backstage, Netflix) converge on "you need either a build graph, a declared catalogue, or an observed trace graph — there is no cheap fourth option," which is a reasonably strong convergence for a stage-1 finding.

**Transfer to an agent.** Transfers as a tiered pipeline rule: if the target repo has a unified build graph (Bazel/Buck/Pants), run reverse-dependency query as the primary impacted-services signal at S1 (cheap, precise, monorepo-only); otherwise, query the service catalogue (Backstage or equivalent) for declared dependencies as the S1 default, and treat trace-derived/observed dependency data as a secondary cross-check where available, flagging catalogue staleness as a declared blind spot per P7 when no catalogue exists. Rubric line: "impacted-services list must cite its method (build-graph query / catalogue lookup / trace observation) and, if none was available, declare 'impacted services unknown' as a named blocker rather than proceeding" — directly serving FM-14.

**Gaps.** Could not fetch the primary Netflix TechBlog post (`netflixtechblog.com`, HTTP 403); relied on a secondary summary instead — the specific quantitative claims (if any) in Netflix's own post were not independently verified. Did not find a primary source on Meta/Buck-based dependency analysis within budget (search results returned mostly Bazel-focused content); RQ text's "Meta on Buck" starting point is therefore an unaddressed gap. Did not find published accuracy or cost figures (false-positive/negative rates, query latency) for any of the four methods — the comparison above is qualitative, not measured.

### RQ-S1-2. How do teams reconstruct why a piece of code exists when its purpose is not obvious, and what do they do when the record is missing?

**Answer.** The primary reconstruction path is git history plus linked-ticket conventions: teams read commit/CL descriptions for the "why" that the code itself cannot express, and Google's own engineering practices explicitly frame this as a defence against breaking Chesterton's fence — a good CL description exists precisely so a later reader is not stuck reverse-engineering intent from the diff alone. Where that record is missing or thin, the two documented fallback practices are architecture decision records (a lightweight, per-decision log capturing context/decision/consequences, proposed by Michael Nygard) written going forward, and characterization tests (Michael Feathers) written retroactively to pin down what legacy code actually does before touching it, standing in for a specification that was never written. No primary source found states a formal organisational policy of the form "do not touch code whose purpose cannot be reconstructed" — the practices found are individual craft discipline (git blame, characterization tests) rather than an enforced gate.

**Patterns found.**
- CL/commit descriptions are written to preserve "why," not just "what," because "reading source code may reveal what the software is doing but it may not reveal why it exists" — [Writing good CL descriptions, Google eng-practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/cl-descriptions.html), primary (official Google engineering practice doc). Explicitly invokes Chesterton's fence by name as the risk of an absent description.
- Architecture Decision Records: Title, Context, Decision, Consequences, written to survive past the point where "no one reads large documents" but the rationale is still needed — [Documenting Architecture Decisions, Michael Nygard / Cognitect](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions), primary (practitioner original source, the founding ADR post). "Small, modular documents have at least a chance at being updated."
- Characterization tests: tests that document actual current behaviour (not intended behaviour) as a safety net before modifying code whose purpose is not fully understood — [Working Effectively with Legacy Code, summarized via understandlegacycode.com](https://understandlegacycode.com/blog/key-points-of-working-effectively-with-legacy-code/), secondary summary of Michael Feathers' book (the primary text itself was not directly fetched).
- Chesterton's fence as the named heuristic for "understand before you remove," with git blame / commit history as the practical tool for applying it when the reasoning isn't otherwise documented — multiple practitioner blogs, e.g. [Chesterton's Fence, stevenallen.dev](https://stevenallen.dev/posts/chestertons_fence/), secondary (individual practitioner posts, not a company engineering blog).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and authoritative for the Google CL-description practice (official, current documentation actively used at Google) and for the ADR origin (Nygard's own post). Feathers' characterization-test pattern is well-established in the industry but represented here only via a secondary summary, not the primary book text. The "what do you do when the record is missing" half of the question is answered only at the level of individual technique (git archaeology, characterization tests) — no source describes an organisational escalation policy for genuinely unrecoverable intent (e.g., "if git blame and the linked ticket both fail, escalate to X").

**Transfer to an agent.** Transfers as a two-step S1 rule: first, attempt reconstruction via git log/blame on the touched lines plus the linked ticket/CL description chain (mirrors Google's own reliance on CL descriptions as the "why" record); second, when that trail is empty or contradictory, the agent should write a characterization test capturing current behaviour rather than guessing intent, and declare the gap explicitly (P4/P7) rather than presuming the code is safe to change. Rubric line: "if git history and linked tickets give no rationale for code being touched, the plan must either cite a new characterization test pinning current behaviour or name this as an explicit unknown requiring human confirmation before the change proceeds."

**Gaps.** Did not fetch Feathers' primary text directly (behind a paywall/book format, not a web page); relied on secondary summary only, consistent with the instruction to flag when only a snippet/summary was available. No source found describing a named organisational policy for handling genuinely unrecoverable code history (all sources describe technique, not escalation policy) — flagged as a gap rather than filled with a plausible generality.

### RQ-S1-3. What does the context section of a strong design document contain, and what do design reviewers refuse to review without?

**Answer.** Google's own canonical account of its design-doc practice (Malte Ubl, who ran the practice at Google) specifies a "Context and scope" section whose job is to give the reader "a very rough overview of the landscape in which the new system is being built and what is actually being built," kept succinct, restricted to objective background facts, and allowed to assume some prior knowledge while linking out to detail. Beyond Google, the same source is explicit that design docs at Google are informal and do not follow a rigid mandatory template — the sections it names (context and scope; goals and non-goals; the actual design; alternatives considered; cross-cutting concerns) are convention, not enforced structure. No primary source found states a published, explicit "reviewers refuse to review without X" rule for design docs specifically; the closest evidence is general design-review practice literature describing reviews as unproductive, or as consuming time reconstructing history, when background/context is missing going in — a pattern rather than a stated refusal policy.

**Patterns found.**
- "Context and scope" as the first substantive section of a Google design doc, purpose "give the reader a very rough overview of the landscape... Keep it succinct! ... This section should be entirely focused on objective background facts" — [Design Docs at Google, industrialempathy.com (Malte Ubl)](https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/), primary (practitioner account by the person who ran the practice at Google; treated as primary practice doc for this research pass).
- Google design docs are informal by design — "design docs are informal documents and thus don't follow a strict guideline for their content" — the named sections (context/scope, goals/non-goals, design, alternatives, cross-cutting concerns) are convention rather than a mandated checklist, and no explicit reviewer-refusal criterion is stated in this source.
- General design-review literature: reviews fail or degrade to "reconstructing the design's history" when shared context (decisions, tradeoffs, rationale) is not brought into the review up front — [Why So Many Design Reviews Fail Before They Start, colabsoftware.com](https://www.colabsoftware.com/post/why-so-many-design-reviews-fail-before-they-start), secondary (vendor practice blog, not software-engineering-specific, but directly on point for the review-failure-mode claim).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and singular for the Google template details (one well-known, widely-cited practitioner account, not independently corroborated by a second primary Google source in this pass). No primary evidence found for Uber, Stripe, Shopify, Squarespace, or Pinterest design-doc/RFC *context-section* templates specifically — Uber's RFC evolution (DUCK format, service SLAs, third-party libraries as fields) was found only at the level of "an RFC template exists and includes fields," not the specific context/background content. The "what reviewers refuse to review without" half of the question has no primary evidence at all; only general, cross-domain secondary material.

**Transfer to an agent.** The Context and scope pattern transfers directly as the required opening section of the S3 plan artefact: a succinct, fact-only background naming current state and constraints, written to let a reviewer judge the design without first reconstructing history — this is exactly the charter's "reconstruction is a defect" contract (section 6) applied to the plan's first section. Rubric line: "the plan's context section states the current state and prior related work in fact-only form, sized so a reviewer unfamiliar with the area can orient in under one minute; a plan with acceptable design content but a missing or padded context section fails the S3 gate on that basis alone."

**Gaps.** Searched for Uber, Stripe, Shopify, Squarespace, and Pinterest's own published design-doc/RFC templates with a specific context/background field; only Uber's RFC *evolution* (not its current template content) was found, and Squarespace/Pinterest searches returned unrelated content (Squarespace the website builder's own background-image docs, not Squarespace engineering). No published source found stating an explicit "reviewers will bounce/refuse a doc lacking field X" policy for software design docs specifically — flagged as no published evidence found rather than filled with the general design-review-literature analogy alone.

### RQ-S1-4. What have practitioners learned about which repository context belongs in standing agent instruction files versus what the agent should discover per task?

**Answer.** Every primary source converges on the same split: standing instruction files (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, Cursor rules, Kiro steering) should hold what the agent cannot infer from the code itself and what must apply on every task — build/test commands, non-default conventions, environment quirks, security/compliance gotchas — while anything the agent can discover by reading the code (file-by-file descriptions, obvious language conventions, standard practices) should be left out, because bloating the file measurably degrades behaviour rather than just costing tokens. Kiro's steering system makes the standing-vs-discovered split a first-class mechanism with three inclusion modes (always-included for universal standards, conditional/glob-scoped for domain-specific guidance, manual/on-demand via `#file` reference for rarely-needed detail), and Cursor's docs give the same shape via Team/Project/User rule layers plus glob-scoped `.mdc` files. The one measured, controlled comparison found (a five-run-per-condition benchmark, not a lab study) shows AGENTS.md producing a 9–27% improvement in wall-time/cost/diff-size depending on task type, with the clearest win coming from eliminating wasted re-orientation and unrequested actions, not from adding more rules — and shows diminishing or negative returns past a length threshold (roughly 200–500 lines depending on tool).

**Patterns found.**
- CLAUDE.md should hold "Bash commands Claude can't guess," "code style rules that differ from defaults," "testing instructions," "repository etiquette," "architectural decisions specific to your project," "developer environment quirks," and "common gotchas" — and explicitly exclude "anything Claude can figure out by reading code," "detailed API documentation," "information that changes frequently," and "self-evident practices like 'write clean code'" — [Best practices for Claude Code, official docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices), primary (official lab documentation). Direct quote: "Bloated CLAUDE.md files cause Claude to ignore your actual instructions!" Domain knowledge relevant only sometimes is explicitly routed to on-demand skills instead of the always-loaded file.
- AGENTS.md as an open standard: nearest-file-wins precedence in monorepos/subprojects, free-form markdown with no required fields beyond what "you'd tell a new teammate" — build/test commands, style, security gotchas, deployment steps — [agents.md official spec site](https://agents.md/), primary (official spec, multi-vendor: OpenAI, Google, Cursor, Factory).
- Cursor's rule guidance explicitly tells authors *not* to duplicate what the agent already knows: "copying entire style guides: use a linter instead," "documenting every possible command: agent knows common tools," "adding instructions for edge cases that rarely apply" — and caps rule length at ~500 lines, with Team → Project → User precedence — [Cursor docs, context/rules](https://cursor.com/docs/context/rules), primary (official tool documentation).
- Kiro steering's three inclusion modes explicitly separate universal standing context (always-included: "core standards that should influence all code generation") from per-task discovery (conditional: loads "only when working with files that match the specified pattern"; manual: "available on-demand by referencing... in chat") — [Steering, Kiro official docs](https://kiro.dev/docs/steering/), primary (official tool documentation). Also recommends treating steering file changes like code changes, requiring review, to prevent staleness.
- Measured benchmark: five runs per condition, two repo clones (with/without a twelve-line AGENTS.md), two tasks. Ambiguous-utility-function task: 27% wall-time reduction, 24% credit reduction, 26% smaller diff with AGENTS.md present. Multi-file task: 9–10% median improvement; without the file, 2 of 5 runs wasted time re-orienting or ran an unrequested production build, versus 0 of 5 with the file — [Measuring AGENTS.md: Five-Run Benchmark Results, Agentic AI Foundation](https://aaif.io/blog/measuring-agents-md-what-five-runs-show-that-one-doesn-t), primary-adjacent (industry-foundation-published practitioner benchmark; single study, not peer-reviewed). Same source: a "protected file" guardrail rule showed no measurable effect because no run in either condition ever touched the protected files — evidence that not every plausible rule changes behaviour.

**Evidence quality.** Primary and multiply-corroborated for the core split (four independent tool vendors — Anthropic, the AGENTS.md working group including OpenAI/Google/Cursor/Factory, Cursor, and AWS/Kiro — all converge on "discoverable-from-code content excluded, non-obvious/always-needed content included, keep it short, nearest/most-specific file wins"). The quantitative claim (9–27% improvement, length thresholds ~200–500 lines) rests on one non-peer-reviewed five-run benchmark from an industry foundation blog, not a controlled academic study or a disclosed internal metric from a lab — treat the direction as well-supported (rot/degradation from bloat is asserted by every vendor independently) but the specific percentages as a single data point, not a validated constant.

**Transfer to an agent.** Transfers directly and is highly actionable: give the factory's own standing instruction file (or CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md itself, since the factory runs on top of these tools per the charter's D5) a hard content test — "would removing this line cause a mistake?" — and route anything conditional or rarely needed to on-demand skills/steering files rather than the always-loaded file. Pipeline rule: cap standing instruction files at roughly 200–500 lines (tool-dependent), require nested/scoped files for domain- or path-specific guidance rather than one growing file, and treat "the agent asked a question already answered in the instruction file" as a signal the file is bloated or ambiguous rather than a signal to add more text — this is the file-staleness/rot half of RQ-X-7 as well and should be cross-referenced there.

### RQ-S1-5. How do organisations use production signals such as SLO status, error budget, and existing feature flags as inputs to planning a change, rather than only to verify it afterwards?

**Answer.** Google's SRE Workbook error-budget-policy chapter describes error budget as a planning input via a hard release gate, not just a retrospective metric: a written, pre-agreed policy specifies that if a service has exceeded its error budget over the trailing four-week window, all changes and releases other than P0 issues or security fixes halt until the service is back within SLO, and a single incident consuming more than 20% of the budget in that window triggers a mandatory postmortem — both are stated as decided in advance, not discovered after the fact. LaunchDarkly's own practice guidance makes the equivalent point for feature flags at design time rather than only at rollout: "the right time to think about whether a feature needs a flag is during the design process," because the presence or absence of a flag changes the feature's design (rollback shape, targeting granularity), not just its deployment mechanics. Both patterns share a structure: a production signal that is normally read as a post-hoc verification metric is instead consulted as a go/no-go or design-shaping input before implementation work starts.

**Patterns found.**
- Error budget policy as an automatic, pre-agreed release gate: "if the service has exceeded its error budget for the preceding four-week window, we will halt all changes and releases other than P0 issues or security fixes until the service is back within its SLO" — [Error Budget Policy, Google SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/), primary (company practice doc). A stated numeric threshold: a single incident consuming more than 20% of the four-week budget mandates a postmortem. Some implementations wire this into deployment pipelines directly, blocking releases when remaining budget falls under a set percentage (25% cited in secondary coverage) — [How to Establish Error Budget Policies for Release Gating on Google Cloud](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-02-17-how-to-establish-error-budget-policies-for-release-gating-on-google-cloud/view), secondary (vendor practice blog interpreting the Google pattern, not itself Google-disclosed).
- Feature-flag design-time planning: "if you think about feature flags during the design process, you will be setting yourself up for success"; whether a flag is short-term or permanent shapes naming, configuration, and review/removal process from the start — [30 Feature Flagging Best Practices Mega Guide, LaunchDarkly](https://launchdarkly.com/guides/30-feature-flagging-best-practices-mega-guide/), primary (official vendor practice doc, LaunchDarkly being the reference implementation for this practice).
- Error budget policy as a mechanism to *permit* rather than only restrict: it "gives teams permission to focus exclusively on reliability when data indicates that reliability is more important than other product features" — same Google SRE Workbook source, primary.

**Evidence quality.** Primary and singular-but-authoritative for the error-budget-as-gate pattern (Google's own SRE Workbook, the canonical source for this practice industry-wide); the "25% threshold in a pipeline" and general release-gating framing is only secondary corroboration, not independently confirmed at a second named organisation. Primary for the flag-at-design-time pattern (LaunchDarkly's own guidance), but LaunchDarkly is a vendor for the practice it is describing, so this is closer to authoritative practitioner guidance than to independent third-party evidence; no additional named organisation (Netflix, Meta) was confirmed within budget describing the same design-time flag practice with specifics.

**Transfer to an agent.** Transfers directly as an S1 context-gathering input, exactly matching the charter's S1 "current SLIs and flags" line in the stage map: before S3 planning starts, the brief should include current error-budget status for the target service (feeding a possible S0/S1 blocker if budget is exhausted, per Google's halt policy) and an inventory of existing flags touching the area being changed (feeding the S3 rollout-strategy section, since an existing flag may change the shape of the safest implementation approach). Rubric line: "the context brief states the target service's current error-budget consumption and lists any existing feature flags in the touched code path; if error budget is exhausted, the plan must either propose a reliability-only change or name the exception reason (P0/security) per the service's own error-budget policy."

**Gaps.** Did not find a primary Netflix or Meta source describing production-readiness-review inputs or design-time flag/SLO consultation specifically (the RQ's suggested starting points); only Google (SRE Workbook) and LaunchDarkly were confirmed with primary material within budget. Did not find a published, named-organisation production readiness review checklist that explicitly lists "current error budget" or "existing flag inventory" as a required input field (as opposed to a general reliability review) — this specific artefact-level evidence is a gap.
