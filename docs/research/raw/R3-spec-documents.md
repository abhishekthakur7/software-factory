### RQ-S3-1. What sections do the strongest design document and RFC templates require, and which sections do reviewers say carry the decision?

**Answer.** Across every company with a published template, four sections are structurally universal: a stated **goals/non-goals or overview**, a **design/architecture** section (by far the longest), an **alternatives-considered** section, and some form of **risk or cross-cutting-concerns** section (security, privacy, operations). Named, dated **approvers with a sign-off mechanism** appear wherever the source discusses review, not just document structure. Reviewers who describe what actually carries the decision point to two different things depending on company: at Google, cross-cutting concerns (security, privacy, observability) are what the review stage is explicitly said to catch; at Squarespace, the decision is made live in a scheduled Architecture Review meeting, not on the page. Primary, checkable templates were found for Google, Uber (partially, via a secondary aggregator), and Squarespace; Stripe, Shopify, and Pinterest do not appear to publish their internal template structure, only process narrative.

**Patterns found.**
- Universal core (goals/non-goals, design, alternatives, risk/cross-cutting) across every template actually located — Design Docs at Google, https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/, practitioner report (author is a former Google staff engineer describing internal practice). Squarespace RFC Template, https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56ab961ecbced617ccd2461e/t/5d792e5a4dac4074658ce64b/1720807430615/Squarespace+RFC+Template.pdf, company practice doc (primary PDF, fetched and read in full).
- Uber's internal templates (distinct services vs. mobile versions) included explicit "Testing & rollout" and "Metrics & monitoring" fields not found in the Google or Squarespace sources — characterized in Software Engineering RFC and Design Doc Examples and Templates, https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/rfcs-and-design-docs/, secondary aggregator (Gergely Orosz), used only to locate/describe Uber's practice since Uber has not published the template itself in an easily locatable primary post.
- Uber's own public OSS RFC template (for the H3 library) is much thinner — RFC/Abstract/Motivation/Approaches/Proposal — showing the internal enterprise template is heavier than what Uber exposes externally. rfc-template.md, https://github.com/uber/h3/blob/master/dev-docs/RFCs/rfc-template.md, company practice doc (primary, but scoped to one open-source project, not representative of Uber's general internal practice).
- Google reviewers are told the review's primary value is catching cross-cutting concerns (security, privacy, observability) early, not defect discovery per se — Design Docs at Google (as above): "ensuring that designs take cross-cutting concerns... into account is something that can be ensured in a review stage," and "the primary value of the review isn't that issues get discovered per-se, but rather that this happens... early."
- At Squarespace, the decision is made in a scheduled, time-boxed meeting (Architecture Review, twice weekly, ~10 participants, 50 minutes discussion + 10 minutes decision), not asynchronously on the document — The Power of "Yes, if": Iterating on our RFC Process, https://engineering.squarespace.com/blog/2019/the-power-of-yes-if, company practice doc (primary).
- Stripe's engineering culture emphasizes sample/reference documents over fill-in-the-blank templates ("engineers aren't forced to stare at a blank page"), and reserves formal pre-publication review for "high-leverage" documents with broad audience impact — How Stripe Built a Writing Culture, https://slab.com/blog/stripe-writing-culture/, secondary (vendor blog built on interviews with Stripe engineers; no Stripe-authored template was located).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for Google (practitioner author, corroborated by the company-authored book *Software Engineering at Google* ch. 10, https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch10.html) and Squarespace (company blog + the actual template PDF). Uber's internal template is known only through a secondary aggregator; its own public template (H3) is primary but narrow. Stripe is characterized only through a secondary interview piece. Shopify and Pinterest yielded no locatable template document at all — the claims made about them in general "RFC lifecycle" round-ups are unverified and were excluded from the table rather than repeated as fact.

**Transfer to an agent.** The four-part universal core (goals/non-goals, design, alternatives, risk/cross-cutting) transfers directly as a minimum required section set for the S3 plan artifact, matching charter S3's "approach, alternatives considered, non-goals... risk map." Rubric line: *a plan fails structural completeness if it lacks goals-and-non-goals, a design/approach section, an alternatives-considered section, and a named risk section — regardless of which company's template it resembles.*

**Gaps.** Searched for Stripe, Shopify, and Pinterest internal design-doc/RFC templates specifically (queries: "Stripe RFC template," "Shopify engineering RFC template," "Pinterest engineering design document template," plus GitHub/gist searches for a public Stripe or Shopify template) and found no company-published template document for any of the three — only process narrative (Stripe) or brief mentions inside third-party round-ups (Shopify) or an unrelated "Experiment Idea Review" one-pager for growth experiments, not general engineering design docs (Pinterest; primary post at https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/how-pinterest-supercharged-its-growth-team-with-experiment-idea-review-fd6571a02fb8 returned HTTP 403 on fetch, so only the search snippet was available). No published evidence found for Shopify's or Pinterest's actual template section list.

### RQ-S3-2. How are design documents evaluated? Are there published review rubrics or checklists, and what causes a design to be sent back?

**Answer.** No company source publishes a scored rubric; what exists instead is **role-based review with a named decision point** — a specific meeting or a specific sign-off field — plus, in open-source governance (Rust, Python), an explicit **status vocabulary and criteria list** that functions as a de facto checklist. Designs are rarely "rejected" outright in the industry sources found; Squarespace explicitly reframes rejection as "not yet" plus named required changes, while Rust and Python have real terminal rejection states with stated grounds (duplication of effort, technical unsoundness, insufficient motivation, incompatibility with project philosophy).

**Patterns found.**
- Google's code review (adjacent practice, same book) splits evaluation into three roles — correctness/understandability (peer LGTM), ownership ("is this right for this part of the codebase"), and readability (language-idiom certification) — a role split that Google also applies to design review via directory ownership. Software Engineering at Google, ch. 9 (Code Review) and ch. 10 (Documentation), https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch09.html and https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch10.html, company-authored book (primary).
- Squarespace's decision venue is Architecture Review: twice-weekly, ~1 hour, ~10 participants (core reviewers + invited domain experts/stakeholders), 50 minutes discussion + 10 minutes explicit decision; the named-approver table in the template (yes / not yet / date) is the recorded artifact of that decision. Rejections are framed as "not yet" with the specific change needed, not outright refusal; the process seeks rough consensus, not unanimity. The Power of "Yes, if", https://engineering.squarespace.com/blog/2019/the-power-of-yes-if, company practice doc (primary).
- Rust RFCs move through a **shepherd** (a trusted developer assigned to keep discussion moving), a mandatory one-week **final comment period** (last call for objections, extendable if the sub-team can't reach consensus), and a **core/sub-team decision** to merge (accept) or close (reject) the PR. 1068-rust-governance, https://rust-lang.github.io/rfcs/1068-rust-governance.html, official project governance doc (primary).
- Python PEPs move through an explicit status vocabulary (Draft, Accepted, Provisional, Final, Rejected, Withdrawn, Deferred, Superseded, Active) decided by a **PEP-Delegate** (a self-nominated core developer, approved by the Steering Council) or the Council itself. Acceptance requires the proposal be a "clear and complete description," "a net improvement," implementable without unduly complicating the interpreter, and "pythonic." Stated rejection grounds: duplication of effort, technical unsoundness, insufficient motivation or failure to address backward compatibility, and inconsistency with Python's philosophy. PEP 1 – PEP Purpose and Guidelines, https://peps.python.org/pep-0001/, official language-governance doc (primary).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for all four (Google's own book, Squarespace's own engineering blog, Rust's and Python's own governance documents). No source in this set publishes a numeric or scored rubric; all describe role/venue structure and, for the OSS governance examples, an explicit accept/reject vocabulary with named grounds. Four independent primary sources agree on the underlying pattern (a designated decision-maker or venue, not a scored checklist).

**Transfer to an agent.** The OSS rejection-grounds vocabulary (duplication, unsoundness, insufficient motivation/compatibility analysis, inconsistency with stated principles) transfers directly as failure categories a plan-review rubric can check against. Rubric line: *a plan is sent back, not silently revised, when it fails one of a named set of grounds (duplicates existing work, technically unsound, doesn't address backward compatibility/migration, or contradicts a stated non-goal) — and the ground is recorded, matching P4's "agent states what it does not know."*

**Gaps.** No published numeric or point-scored design-review rubric was found at any of the four companies; searched "design review rubric," "design doc checklist Google," "RFC review checklist" without finding a scored instrument, only role/process descriptions. This is consistent with — not contradicted by — the S5/S6 cross-cutting question on LLM-as-judge rubrics (RQ-X-5, RQ-S5-3), which is likely where a scored rubric would need to be *invented* rather than borrowed.

### RQ-S3-3. How do Shape Up appetite and no-gos, design doc non-goals, and PR size norms contain scope, and what measured evidence links change size to review time or defect rate?

**Answer.** All three scope-containment mechanisms work the same way: they convert an open-ended "how good could this be" question into a bounded one before work starts, either by fixing time and letting scope flex (Shape Up appetite), by explicitly naming what will not be addressed (non-goals, no-gos), or by capping the unit reviewers see (small-CL norms). The quantitative evidence is unusually strong and converges from three independent large-sample industry studies: **Cisco/SmartBear** (2,500 reviews, 3.2M LOC, 50 developers) found defect density and review effectiveness both collapse above roughly 200–400 lines; **Google** (9 million reviewed changes, Jan 2014–Jul 2016) found a median change size of 24 lines and review latency that roughly quintuples from small to very large changes; **Microsoft** (1.5 million comments across 190,050 reviews, 5 projects) found comment usefulness density falls as the number of files in a change rises. All three studies independently support smaller change size as causally or at least strongly associated with better review outcomes.

**Patterns found.**
- **Shape Up appetite** fixes the time budget first (Small Batch: 1–2 weeks; Big Batch: 6 weeks for a standard team) and treats scope as the only free variable: "the appetite forces you to make decisions and trade-offs, and the flexible scope allows you to make those decisions by letting you remove any tasks or features." **No-gos** are explicit exclusions named in the pitch — "functionality or use cases intentionally not being covered to fit the appetite." Shape Up, https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03 (and ch. 6, "Write the Pitch"), company/practitioner book (primary, Basecamp/37signals).
- **Google's small-CL guidance**: reviewers "have discretion to reject your change outright for the sole reason of it being too large"; a CL should be "one self-contained change" addressing "just one thing." Small CLs, https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html, company practice doc (primary).
- **Cisco/SmartBear study** (10-month, in-situ, 2,500 reviews / 3.2M LOC / 50 developers; final analysis after excluding 21% of reviews as non-representative, n≈1,975): average defect density 32 defects/kLOC; 61% of reviews found zero defects; **no review larger than 250 lines produced more than 37 defects/kLOC** (vs. much higher rates below 200 lines); reviewers slower than 400 LOC/hour were above-average at finding defects, while faster than 450 LOC/hour they were below-average in **87% of cases**; overall defect rate 13 defects/hour, with **85% of reviews slower than 25 defects/hour** and **94% of reviews under 20 defects/hour regardless of size**; the paper's recommendation: review 100–300 LOC at a time, 30–60 minutes, never exceeding 90 minutes or 400 LOC. Code Review at Cisco Systems (in *Best Kept Secrets of Peer Code Review*), https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf, industry practitioner study (primary; full text read).
- **Google (Sadowski et al. 2018)**: dataset of **~9 million reviewed changes** by 25,000+ authors/reviewers (Jan 2014–Jul 2016); **median change size 24 lines**; >10% of changes touch a single line; >35% touch a single file; ~90% touch fewer than 10 files; median review latency **under 4 hours overall — under 1 hour for small changes vs. ~5 hours for very large changes**; average comments per change **peaks at 12.5 for ~1,250-line changes**, then declines for still-larger changes (mostly auto-generated code/large deletions). Compared to other orgs in the cited Rigby & Bird data: AMD median 44 lines, Lucent 263 lines; median time-to-approval 17.5h (AMD), 15.7h (Chrome OS), 14.7–19.8h (three Microsoft projects); a separate Microsoft study (Czerwonka et al. 2015) found median time-to-approval of 24 hours. Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google, https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf, ICSE-SEIP 2018 peer-reviewed paper (primary; full text read).
- **Microsoft (Bosu, Greiler & Bird 2015)**: **~1.5 million comments** classified across **190,050 review requests** from 5 projects (Azure, Bing, Visual Studio, Exchange, Office); overall "usefulness density" 65.5% (range 63.3–68.6% by project); usefulness density **falls as the number of files in a change grows** (trend from roughly high-60s% down toward low-60s% as file count rises past ~20–40 files); reviewers with prior review experience on a file were "almost twice" as useful (65–71%) as first-time reviewers (32–37%). Characteristics of Useful Code Reviews: An Empirical Study at Microsoft, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/bosu2015useful.pdf, MSR 2015 peer-reviewed paper (primary; full text read).

**Evidence quality.** All three quantitative studies are primary and were read in full (not summarized secondhand): an industry case study with a stated methodology and explicit sample-exclusion rules (Cisco/SmartBear), and two peer-reviewed empirical software-engineering papers (Google ICSE-SEIP 2018; Microsoft MSR 2015). All three are measured, not anecdotal, and all three independently converge on the same direction of effect (larger change size → worse review outcome), despite different organizations, tools, and eras (2006, 2015, 2018). This is one of the strongest evidence clusters in this packet.

**Transfer to an agent.** Appetite/no-gos and non-goals both transfer as the plan-time mechanism (charter already requires "non-goals" and P5); the quantitative studies transfer as the basis for a **hard size gate** on generated diffs. Rubric line: *a generated change over ~300–400 lines (or touching more than ~10 files) triggers a mandatory split-or-justify step before S6 review, citing the Cisco (defect density collapses above 250–400 LOC) and Microsoft (usefulness density falls with file count) findings as the rationale.*

**Gaps.** No numeric defect-rate figures were located specifically attributable to a Google-internal study beyond Sadowski et al.'s latency/size figures (Google does not appear to publish a defect-density-by-size number the way Cisco/SmartBear or Microsoft do); Sadowski et al. instead cite qualitative agreement ("a correlation between change size and review quality is acknowledged by Google"). Treat the "Google" leg of this evidence set as latency/comment-count evidence, not defect-density evidence.

### RQ-S3-4. What do design and planning practices require a plan to say about testing and about rollout before it is approved?

**Answer.** On testing, the clearest published standard is Google's **test-size taxonomy** (small/medium/large, defined by execution scope and resource access, not line count) plus a stated target mix of roughly 80% small / 15% medium / 5% large tests. On rollout, the clearest published standard is Google SRE's **launch checklist**, which requires a plan to name concrete rollout steps with assigned ownership and per-step contingency/risk measures, organized under nine categories including an explicit "Rollout Planning" category. Neither source publishes a plan-approval gate that blocks on these sections by name; company RFC/design-doc templates found in this packet (Squarespace) fold testing and rollout expectations into more general "Operations" and "Risks" sections rather than naming them as required, separately-labeled sections.

**Patterns found.**
- **Google test sizes**: small tests run single-process/single-thread with no I/O, network, or blocking calls; medium tests may span processes/threads and call `localhost` only; large tests remove the localhost restriction and may span machines/clusters, reserved for full end-to-end and legacy validation. Target distribution: ~80% unit / 15% integration / 5% end-to-end "by count." Every change is expected to include both feature code and tests, and "it is perfectly reasonable to block a change if it is missing tests" — though no formal test-plan template is specified. Software Engineering at Google, ch. 11 (Testing Overview), https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html, company-authored book (primary).
- **Google SRE launch checklist** (Launch Coordination Engineers): nine categories — Architecture and Dependencies, Integration, Capacity Planning, Failure Modes, Client Behavior, Processes and Automation, Development Process, External Dependencies, and **Rollout Planning**. The Rollout Planning category requires the plan to name "actions to take to launch the service" with assigned ownership and to identify "risk in the individual launch steps and implement contingency measures." Checklist items are meant to be justified "ideally by a previous launch disaster," i.e., traceable to a real failure mode, not generic. Reliable Product Launches (ch. 27), https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/, Google SRE book (primary).
- **Squarespace RFC template** requires "Operations" (new regular processes, who runs the system) and "Risks" (security, complexity, compatibility, latency, service immaturity, team expertise) but has no separately labeled "testing strategy" or "rollout/flags/rollback" section — those concerns are expected to be folded into Design, Operations, and Risks at the author's discretion. Squarespace RFC Template (as cited under RQ-S3-1), primary PDF.

**Evidence quality.** Primary for all three (Google's own book for testing, Google's own SRE book for rollout, Squarespace's own template for the negative case — i.e., what a real template does *not* require by name). No cross-company corroboration was found for the specific claim that a plan is *blocked from approval* without a named testing or rollout section; the evidence supports what mature practices *ask reviewers to check for*, not a hard gate.

**Transfer to an agent.** The test-size taxonomy transfers directly as a required field in the S3 plan's "test strategy" (charter S3 already lists this): the agent should state, per behavior, which test size it is targeting and why. Rubric line: *a plan's test-strategy section must classify each planned test by scope (single-process / single-machine-localhost-only / full-system) and state the target mix, and a rollout section must name each rollout step, its owner, and its contingency — modeled on Google SRE's Rollout Planning category — even though Squarespace's own template shows this is not yet universal industry practice.*

**Gaps.** No published "production readiness gate" that formally blocks approval absent a named testing/rollout section was found at any single company (Google's checklist is described as consulted and curated, not a hard pre-launch veto with binary pass/fail criteria); searched "production readiness review checklist gate" and "design doc template testing section required" without finding a source that states the gate is enforced rather than advisory.

### RQ-S3-5. What do AI labs and agent tool vendors say about the plan artefact: its content, its review, and whether reviewing plans reduces rework?

**Answer.** Anthropic, Cursor, and Kiro (AWS) each publish an explicit plan-then-implement workflow with the plan as a distinct, human-reviewable artifact gating implementation; none of the three publishes measured evidence that reviewing the plan reduces rework — the justification given is uniformly qualitative ("avoid solving the wrong problem," faster to redo a plan than fix an in-progress build). OpenAI/Codex does not appear to publish an equivalent named "plan mode" documentation page as of this research pass, despite community requests for one.

**Patterns found.**
- **Anthropic (Claude Code)**: official guidance names a four-phase workflow — **Explore → Plan → Implement → Commit**. In Explore, Claude is read-only. In Plan, the user asks for "a detailed implementation plan," can open it in an editor (Ctrl+G) to hand-edit before implementation proceeds, and only then switches out of plan mode by explicit approval. Stated rationale: "Letting Claude jump straight to coding can produce code that solves the wrong problem," and planning is recommended when "you're uncertain about the approach, when the change modifies multiple files, or when you're unfamiliar with the code" — explicitly skippable ("if you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan"). A related pattern in the same doc: for larger features, "have Claude interview you first" via `AskUserQuestion` and write a spec file before implementation, with the claim that "time spent making the spec precise pays off more than time spent watching the implementation" (stated, not measured). Best practices for Claude Code, https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices, lab documentation (primary).
- **Cursor**: Plan Mode has the agent research the codebase and ask clarifying questions, then produce a plan reviewable and editable "through chat or markdown files" before the user clicks to build. Stated rationale is efficiency, not measured rework reduction: reverting and refining a plan "is often faster than fixing an in-progress agent." Plan Mode, https://cursor.com/docs/agent/plan-mode, tool-vendor documentation (primary).
- **Kiro (AWS)**: three sequential phases — Requirements (requirements.md, EARS-notation acceptance criteria) → Design (design.md, architecture/sequence diagrams) → Tasks (tasks.md, discrete executable tasks) — with two workflow variants ("Requirements-First," "Design-First") that both pass through named **approval gates** between phases; a separate "Quick Spec" mode explicitly bypasses the gates by auto-generating all three artifacts. No quantitative claim about rework or quality was found on this page. Specs, https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/, tool-vendor documentation (primary).
- **OpenAI Codex**: no dedicated "plan mode" documentation page was located on OpenAI's own developer docs (checked the Codex CLI, developer-commands, and configuration pages); an open community feature-request/discussion thread exists asking for one ("Plan / Spec Mode," GitHub Discussion #7355), and the Codex CLI quickstart mentions "plan a change" only as a general activity within the interactive loop, not as a named, separately documented mode. Codex CLI docs, https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/codex/cli, lab documentation (primary, but confirms absence rather than presence); Plan / Spec Mode, https://github.com/openai/codex/discussions/7355, community discussion (secondary/practitioner, cited only to show the gap is noticed by users).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and current (official docs) for Anthropic, Cursor, and Kiro's process description. Zero of the four sources provide measured evidence (before/after rework rates, revision counts, or review-time deltas) that plan review specifically reduces rework — every justification found is a designer's stated rationale, not a study. This matches the pattern already found industry-wide in RQ-S3-2/S3-3: process description is well published, causal measurement of the review step itself is not.

**Transfer to an agent.** This is the closest published analogue to the charter's own S3 design (plan artifact, human approves-or-redirects, exit criterion = "plan passes the rubric and is approved") — Anthropic's explore→plan→implement→commit sequence and Kiro's requirements→design→tasks with approval gates both map almost directly onto the charter's S2→S3→S4 split. Rubric line: *the plan must be produced and approved as a distinct artifact before any implementation tool call runs (matching Claude Code plan mode and Kiro's approval gates), and the plan-approval step itself should be instrumented from day one (revisions-per-ticket, per charter section 8) since no existing lab or vendor source supplies a rework-reduction baseline to borrow.*

**Gaps.** No published quantitative evidence was found, from any of the four vendors, that plan review measurably reduces rework, revision cycles, or defect rates — searched "Anthropic plan mode rework evidence," "Claude Code plan review outcomes study," and checked OpenAI's own Codex documentation pages directly; none published such a figure. No official OpenAI Codex "plan mode" documentation page was found as of this research pass (Sept 2026) — the capability may exist without a named/documented mode, or documentation may have moved; this should be re-checked before being cited as a settled absence. (Note: this sub-question's search depth was constrained by the session's shared web-search budget being exhausted partway through this packet; remaining lookups relied on direct URL fetches rather than fresh searches.)

### RQ-S3-6. How do organizations record alternatives considered and rejected, and is there evidence that the record helps later readers?

**Answer.** Two converging formats dominate: the **design-doc "Alternatives Considered" section** (Google, Squarespace) that sits inside the main proposal document, and the **ADR "considered options" format**, descending from Michael Nygard's original Context/Decision/Status/Consequences template and extended by MADR into an explicit Considered-Options → Pros-and-Cons-per-option → Decision-Outcome structure. The stated rationale for both is the same and is *asserted*, not measured: a written record keeps "the motivation behind previous decisions... visible for everyone, present and future," preventing both blind re-litigation and blind adherence to stale decisions. No published empirical study measuring whether these records are actually reused by later readers, or how that reuse affects outcomes, was located in this pass.

**Patterns found.**
- Google's design doc template includes an explicit "Alternatives considered" section: "details competing approaches and their trade-offs." Design Docs at Google, https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/, practitioner report (as cited under RQ-S3-1).
- Squarespace's template includes "Alternatives Considered/Prior Art," directly asking "What other approaches did you consider? What existing solutions are close but not quite right?" — explicitly framed to force integration-vs-replacement thinking about prior art, not just internal options. Squarespace RFC Template, primary PDF (as cited under RQ-S3-1).
- Nygard's original ADR format (Title, Context, Decision, Status, Consequences) does not itself have a dedicated "alternatives" field, but the "Consequences" section is meant to capture trade-offs of the decision actually made, and Nygard's stated purpose is explicitly to prevent two failure modes: blindly accepting an old decision when context has changed, and blindly reversing it without understanding why it was made. Documenting Architecture Decisions, https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions, practitioner blog (primary; original source of the ADR pattern).
- MADR (Markdown Any Decision Records) extends Nygard's format with an explicit **"Considered Options"** list, a **"Pros and Cons of the Options"** section per alternative (Good/Bad/Neutral, with reasons), and a **"Decision Outcome"** section naming the chosen option and its justification — directly operationalizing "alternatives considered and rejected" as a structured, per-option record rather than prose. MADR template, https://adr.github.io/madr/, community/practitioner standard (primary source for the format itself).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for all four sources (original practitioner blog for ADRs, the community-maintained MADR spec, and the two company templates already verified under RQ-S3-1). All claims about *why* this helps are the format designers' stated rationale — anecdotal/asserted, not measured. Two independent formats (design-doc section, ADR-family) converge on the same underlying claim, which is some corroboration of the pattern's perceived value, but not of its measured effect.

**Transfer to an agent.** This maps directly onto charter P4 ("agent states what it does not know") and P9 (plan/decisions as the reviewable unit): an agent-authored plan should carry both a design-doc-style Alternatives section for the change as a whole and, for any individually consequential decision inside it, an ADR-style Considered-Options/Decision-Outcome micro-record. Rubric line: *every rejected alternative recorded in the plan must state, in one line each, what it was and why it was rejected (MADR's "Bad, because..." pattern), so a later reviewer or agent can distinguish "not considered" from "considered and rejected for reason X" — directly serving P4 and the charter's "deliberately not touched" requirement.*

**Gaps.** No published empirical study measuring whether ADRs or alternatives sections are actually consulted by later readers, or whether their presence changes later decisions, rework, or onboarding time, was located in this pass. This sub-question's search was constrained by the session's web-search budget being exhausted before it could be pursued with fresh queries beyond the two direct-URL fetches (Nygard's post, the MADR spec); a dedicated follow-up search for academic literature on ADR *usage/reuse* (as opposed to ADR *adoption*) is warranted and was not completed here.
