### RQ-X-1. What is known about the cost of context switching and interruption for engineers, and about batching decisions to reduce it?

**Answer.** The best-supported finding is not that interruptions add a fixed delay, but that they change *how* work gets done: people compensate for interruptions by working faster, at a measurable cost in stress, frustration, effort, and workload — and separately, resuming an interrupted *programming* task specifically carries a real time cost because the programmer has to rebuild code-location context before the first productive edit. The popular "23 minutes to refocus" figure has no traceable peer-reviewed source and should not be cited. For programmers specifically, only 10% of interrupted sessions resume coding activity within a minute, and about 30% take longer than 30 minutes, with most of that time spent navigating rather than editing. Developers self-report "few interruptions" as the second most common reason (50.4% of respondents) for a productive day, yet observed developers switch tasks roughly every 6 minutes and activities every 1.6 minutes and still feel productive — the cost depends heavily on whether the switch is self-inflicted/short or externally imposed/cognitively distant from the current task. Batching — delivering interruptions at scheduled intervals instead of continuously — is separately shown to reduce the well-being and attention cost of interruptions in general knowledge work, though not in a software-engineering-specific study.

**Patterns found.**
- Interrupted work is completed *faster*, not slower, but at a significant cost in stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort (NASA-TLX subscales all p<.05 or better) — "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress," Mark, Gudith & Klocke, CHI 2008, https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf, peer-reviewed paper (primary, read in full). Lab experiment, n=48 (German university students), interruption frequency fixed at every 2 minutes; after only 20 minutes of interrupted work, participants reported significantly higher stress, frustration, workload, effort, and time pressure than an uninterrupted baseline.
- Programmers resume coding within 1 minute of an interruption in only 10% of sessions; roughly 30% of sessions have an "edit lag" over 30 minutes; when the last-edited method is reused as a resumption cue it works well (35% resume within 1 minute) but that cue is only available/used in 17% of sessions — "Resumption Strategies for Interrupted Programming Tasks," Parnin & Rugaber, ICPC 2009, https://chrisparnin.me/pdf/parnin-icpc09.pdf, peer-reviewed paper (primary, read in full). Analysis of 9,899 recorded IDE sessions from 85 professional/industrial programmers (Eclipse + Visual Studio interaction logs). The paper also cites van Solingen et al. (IEEE Software, 1998), an industrial study finding software companies lost roughly an hour a day per developer to interruptions, with a typical 15-minute recovery time (secondary citation found inside the primary paper, not independently verified).
- 50.4% of 379 surveyed professional developers named "no/few interruptions and distractions" as a top-five reason for a productive workday (2nd most common, after "complete tasks/goals" at 53.2%); an observational study of 11 developers over 44 hours found a mean task-switch rate of 13.3/hour and activity-switch rate of 47.0/hour, yet 8 of 11 (73%) still rated their observed session as productive — cost depends on switch type: self-inflicted and short switches (e.g., a quick email) are cheap, externally imposed switches to a cognitively different task are expensive — "Software Developers' Perceptions of Productivity," Meyer, Fritz, Murphy & Zimmermann, FSE 2014, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/meyer-fse-2014.pdf, peer-reviewed paper co-authored by Microsoft Research (primary, read in full).
- "A single meeting can devastate a maker's schedule" — a meeting placed in the middle of a work block breaks it into two pieces "each too small to do anything hard in," and knowing a meeting is coming dampens the will to start deep work beforehand — "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule," Paul Graham, 2009, https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html, practitioner essay (primary, read). Anecdotal, not measured, but foundational to how the industry talks about protecting blocks of attention.
- Batching notifications into three fixed daily intervals (vs. continuous delivery) led to participants feeling more attentive, more productive, and less stressed — "Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being," Fitz et al., Computers in Human Behavior, 2019, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596, peer-reviewed paper (secondary — found via search summary and publisher abstract page only; full text was paywalled and not independently read). Randomized field experiment, n=237. Not software-engineering-specific.
- The "23 minutes 15 seconds to refocus after an interruption" figure widely repeated in productivity writing traces to an unpublished 2006 Gallup interview with Gloria Mark and does not appear in any of the peer-reviewed papers commonly cited for it — noted via "The Famous '23 Minutes 15 Seconds' Interruption Recovery Time Has No Scientific Paper Behind It," BigGo Finance, https://finance.biggo.com/news/202508240713_23_Minutes_Interruption_Recovery_Myth, secondary/journalistic source. Included here as a correction, since the charter's FM-09 language ("fresh deep-attention session") risks being anchored to this unsupported figure.

**Evidence quality.** Primary and measured for the three peer-reviewed studies (Mark et al., Parnin & Rugaber, Meyer et al.), each independently conducted (different labs, different methods — lab experiment, IDE-log mining, survey+observation) and converging on the same qualitative conclusion: interruption cost is real but is not a single fixed number, and self-report differs from observed behavior. The Graham essay is anecdotal/primary-practitioner. The batching study is peer-reviewed but not fully read (paywalled) and not domain-specific to software engineering — treat as suggestive, not proven for this population.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves FM-09. Rubric/pipeline rule: never deliver a single agent question or review request as an interrupt into an active coding session — always route through a queue and let the human choose when to open it, matching the charter's existing "Delivery" and "Batching and ranking" rules in section 6. A second, narrower rule: track "resumption cost" as a distinct signal from "question count" — a ticket that raises three questions delivered together costs less human attention than three tickets each raising one question at a random time, even though the raw count is identical.

**Gaps.** Searched for a software-engineering-specific study of *batching* interruptions/reviews (as opposed to general knowledge-work notification batching) and found none — the Fitz et al. study is the closest available evidence and is about smartphone notifications generally. Also searched for the primary academic source of the "23-minute" claim and found none exists (documented above as a correction).

### RQ-X-2. How do organisations run asynchronous decision processes: written narratives, comment periods, lazy consensus, decision-by-default with objection windows?

**Answer.** Mature async-first organisations converge on a common shape: a written proposal, a fixed or open-ended objection window during which anyone can raise a substantive objection, and a default of "proceed if nobody objects" rather than requiring active sign-off from every stakeholder. The variations are in how the window is bounded (Apache's is informal/context-dependent, ~72 hours as a norm; Rust's is a fixed 10-calendar-day Final Comment Period) and in how heavily the proposal itself is front-loaded (Amazon's six-page narrative memo, read silently for 20-30 minutes before any discussion). GitLab's variant adds an explicit synchronous escape hatch: after three async round-trips fail to converge, move to a live call.

**Patterns found.**
- Lazy consensus: announce intent publicly, proceed without waiting for explicit approval, but be prepared to roll back if someone raises a legitimate objection; silence counts as agreement; a typical wait window is informally ~72 hours — "Lazy Consensus," Apache Community Development, https://community.apache.org/committers/decisionMaking.html, organisation practice doc (primary). "It's easier for people to agree by doing nothing than to object, which requires them to propose an alternative" — this is stated explicitly as the mechanism's design intent, not an accident.
- Rust RFC Final Comment Period (FCP): once a subteam believes it has enough information to decide, all subteam members must sign off before entering FCP; FCP lasts 10 calendar days (guaranteeing at least 5 business days) and is advertised widely (e.g., in "This Week in Rust") so all stakeholders can object before the decision (merge/close/postpone) is finalized — "RFC Process," rust-lang/rfcs README, https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/blob/master/README.md, official tool-governance doc (primary).
- GitLab defaults to async, written, public-by-default communication (issues, merge requests, the handbook itself as the single source of truth); escalates to synchronous only after roughly three async back-and-forths fail to converge — "The complete guide to asynchronous and non-linear working," GitLab Handbook, https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/non-linear-workday/, company practice doc (primary).
- Amazon's six-page narrative memo replaced PowerPoint for major decisions; meetings open with 20-30 minutes of silent reading so every participant has engaged with the full argument before discussion starts, and the memo is often paired with an internal press release ("Working Backwards") that forces the proposal to be framed from the customer's perspective before the decision is made — "Amazon Narratives — Memos, Working Backwards from Release, More," Andreessen Horowitz podcast summary, https://a16z.com/podcast/amazon-narratives-memos-working-backwards-from-release-more/, secondary summary of Bryar & Carr's *Working Backwards* (book not independently fetched in this pass).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and consistent across three independent official practice/governance docs (Apache, Rust, GitLab) — all peer open-source or company sources, not commentary. The Amazon material is secondary (podcast/blog coverage of the *Working Backwards* book); the book itself is a practitioner account, not measured, and was not fetched directly in this pass.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves FM-09 and FM-07. Pipeline rule: implement the "propose, publish, default-to-proceed-after-window, log the objection if any" pattern for accepted-default questions — this maps exactly onto the charter's "Defaults" rule (accepting a default costs one word) and "Rounds" rule (follow-ups name the answer that raised them). A second rule: front-load the proposal itself, Amazon-style — the S3 plan should be dense and self-contained enough that a human can read it in one sitting and form a judgment before any discussion, rather than requiring back-and-forth to extract the reasoning.

**Gaps.** Did not independently fetch or read *Working Backwards* (Bryar & Carr) or the primary Amazon PR/FAQ template; relied on secondary coverage. Did not find Shopify's specific async RFC process documentation (named in the question's starting sources) within budget — searched "Shopify async RFC practice" indirectly via the general GitLab/async query but did not locate a distinct Shopify primary source.

### RQ-X-3. What have labs and vendors published about humans supervising multiple concurrent agents, and what did they learn about review load and the interaction surface?

**Answer.** The consistent finding across Anthropic, Cognition (Devin), and (to a lesser extent) Cursor and OpenAI Codex is that as agent parallelism scales up, the constraint shifts away from the agents' raw throughput and onto the human's "management, planning, and reviewing" load — Cognition states this almost verbatim. The published responses are architectural, not procedural: build a coordination/manager layer that synthesizes multiple agents' work before it reaches the human, rather than exposing N raw diffs for a human to review one-by-one. Anthropic's own multi-agent system also reports that oversight in practice is largely post-hoc pattern analysis rather than real-time supervisory control, and that their orchestrator-worker pattern works well for parallelizable research but explicitly not for tightly interdependent coding work.

**Patterns found.**
- "When you are using so many agents, you naturally start to become bottlenecked on everything around those agents: the management, planning, and reviewing" — Cognition's fix is architectural: keep writes single-threaded, have additional agents "contribute intelligence rather than actions," and use a manager agent that coordinates and synthesizes child agents' work rather than requiring the human to arbitrate each one — "Multi-Agents: What's Actually Working," Cognition, https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working, company engineering blog (primary).
- Devin's manager pattern: a manager Devin decomposes a large task and spawns child Devins, each in its own isolated VM with a "clean slate, narrow focus"; the human engineer's role shifts from implementer to reviewer of PRs and high-level decisions, not tracker of each child agent — "Devin can now Manage Devins," Cognition, https://cognition.ai/blog/devin-can-now-manage-devins, company engineering blog (primary, via search summary).
- Anthropic's orchestrator-worker research system: a lead agent plans and spins up 3-5 subagents in parallel, achieving a 90.2% performance improvement over a single agent on an internal eval, but at roughly 15x the token cost of a single chat interaction; the pattern is explicitly recommended for "breadth-first" research questions that decompose into independent parallel strands and explicitly *not* recommended for "tightly interdependent tasks such as coding" — "How we built our multi-agent research system," Anthropic, https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system, company engineering blog (primary). The lead agent executes subagents synchronously (waiting for completion, no mid-task steering); human oversight is described as post-hoc pattern/decision-structure analysis rather than live intervention, explicitly to preserve privacy and manage emergent, hard-to-predict subagent behavior changes.
- OpenAI Codex runs each task in its own isolated cloud sandbox with no internet access during execution, and explicitly supports several engineers each queuing tasks in parallel; the interaction surface is a PR per task, reviewed by a human afterward — "Introducing Codex," OpenAI, https://openai.com/index/introducing-codex/, vendor product announcement (primary, via search summary; borderline vendor-marketing but describes a concrete architectural mechanism, not a claim).
- Cursor's explicit review discipline for background agents: "Always review every PR. Even when the agent says it's done, always read the diff. Agents can introduce subtle logic errors or miss edge cases that only a human familiar with the codebase would catch" — this is a manual, per-diff review norm with no load-balancing mechanism described across many parallel agents — cited via secondary practitioner write-ups summarizing Cursor's own docs/blog (not independently fetched from cursor.com in this pass).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and independently converging for the two strongest sources (Anthropic, Cognition) — both are engineering blogs from labs directly building this capability, and both name the same underlying problem (review/coordination load, not agent capability) using different vocabulary. OpenAI and Cursor evidence is weaker: mostly product-announcement or secondary-summarized rather than a first-party retrospective on review load specifically.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves FM-09 and is arguably the single strongest cross-industry validation of the charter's P1 framing (human attention, not agent throughput, is the constraint). Pipeline rule: the factory should never present N independent agent outputs to a human as N separate review events — a synthesis/manager layer should merge, deduplicate, and risk-rank before the human sees anything, mirroring the charter's existing "risk map naming the three places worth a reviewer's eyes" in the review packet (section 6). A second, narrower rule sourced from Anthropic: reserve multi-agent parallelism for genuinely independent subtasks (e.g., parallel context-gathering across services in S1) and avoid it for tightly coupled implementation work in S4, where a single agent with a clear plan outperforms a swarm.

**Gaps.** Did not find published material from Google Jules specifically (named in the question's starting sources) on review load or supervision within the search budget used. Did not find quantitative data (e.g., "N agents per reviewer before quality drops") from any vendor — all sources are qualitative/architectural, not measured.

### RQ-X-4. What is known about propose-a-default, human-approves interaction patterns and their acceptance rates?

**Answer.** The two best-documented industrial deployments — Google's ML-suggested code-review edits and Meta's Getafix/SapFix automated bug-fix pipeline — both show a similar shape: roughly half of proposals that are actually surfaced to a human and reviewed get accepted, but the surfaced/previewed fraction is itself a small slice of all possible opportunities, so the end-to-end "resolved via the proposed default" rate against the total addressable volume is much lower (single digits to low tens of a percent). GitHub's Copilot Autofix enforces mandatory human review before any AI-proposed fix is applied but does not publish an acceptance rate.

**Patterns found.**
- In full production deployment, Google's ML-suggested edit resolves 7.5% of *all* reviewer comments left across the company (tens of millions of comments/year); of comments where a suggestion is shown to the author, 40-50% of previewed suggestions are applied; a UI change (a prominent "Show ML-edit" button) roughly doubled the preview rate to ~40% and, with it, doubled the overall resolved fraction from beta to full launch — "Resolving code review comments with ML," Google Research, https://research.google/blog/resolving-code-review-comments-with-ml/, company research blog (primary, fetched directly). Offline model precision is calibrated to a 50% target, addressing 52% of comments at that precision bar. Google states the expected impact is hundreds of thousands of engineer-hours saved annually.
- Meta's Getafix generates fix candidates for bugs found by Sapienz (dynamic testing) and Infer (static analysis); about 80% of Getafix's candidates pass all automated tests before being handed to SapFix, but only about half of the fixes SapFix actually proposes are accepted as good by the human engineer who reviews them; SapFix cannot deploy its own fixes — a human is always the final gate — "Getafix: How Facebook tools learn to fix bugs automatically," Engineering at Meta, https://engineering.fb.com/2018/11/06/developer-tools/getafix-how-facebook-tools-learn-to-fix-bugs-automatically/, company engineering blog (primary, via search summary).
- GitHub Copilot Autofix requires explicit developer review and acceptance before any suggested fix is applied to a CodeQL alert, and GitHub explicitly warns reviewers to "always consider the limitations of AI and edit the changes as needed"; coverage is stated (an expanded set of CodeQL alert types covers 29% of all CodeQL alerts) but no acceptance-rate figure is published — "About Copilot Autofix for code scanning," GitHub Docs, https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/concepts/code-scanning/copilot-autofix-for-code-scanning, official product doc (primary).

**Evidence quality.** Primary and measured for both Google and Meta, from first-party company engineering sources describing their own production systems — these are the two strongest data points in the whole packet. They agree qualitatively (roughly half of *reviewed* proposals are accepted) despite being unrelated systems (code-review-comment suggestions vs. automated bug fixes), which is a meaningful convergence. GitHub's figure is a documented gap, not an estimate.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves FM-07 and P1. Pipeline rule: track two separate acceptance metrics, not one — (a) the surfacing/preview rate (what fraction of possible defaults the agent even proposes with enough confidence to show a human) and (b) the accept-given-shown rate (what fraction of shown proposals a human approves) — because collapsing them into a single "acceptance rate" hides whether the system's bottleneck is confidence calibration or default quality. This maps directly onto the charter's own success measure, "Questions per ticket reaching a human... and the share resolved by accepting the default" (section 8) — the Google/Meta data suggests a healthy accept-given-shown rate for a mature system sits near 40-50%, which is a useful calibration anchor once the factory has pilot data.

**Gaps.** Did not find a published acceptance-rate figure for GitHub Copilot Autofix specifically (documented above as a gap, not filled with an estimate). Did not find data from a source restricted purely to *plan-level* propose-a-default patterns (as opposed to code-level fix suggestions) — both strong data points are about code/comment edits, not about proposed defaults for requirements or design questions; this is a gap relative to how RQ-X-4 would ideally apply to the charter's S2/S3 stages.

### RQ-X-5. How do labs design rubrics for grading subjective engineering artefacts such as plans, specs, and reviews, and how do they calibrate against humans?

**Answer.** Anthropic's own guidance is to split a rubric into single-dimension judges rather than one judge grading everything at once, to calibrate each judge against expert human ratings before trusting it (especially for genuinely subjective work like research quality), to test the rubric on known-good/known-bad examples before production use, and to give the judge an explicit "Unknown" escape hatch to reduce confident-but-wrong verdicts. Separately, methodological research shows that reported human-LLM agreement is highly sensitive to protocol choices that have nothing to do with judgment quality — the same underlying verdicts can produce accuracy figures that differ by nearly 35 points depending only on how abstentions and aggregation are handled — meaning a bare "our judge agrees with humans" claim is not trustworthy without a disclosed, fixed measurement protocol. Well-designed, atomic rubric items can achieve very high inter-judge reliability (Cohen's κ near 1.0 across reruns), but this appears to be a property of good rubric design specifically, not a default property of LLM judges.

**Patterns found.**
- "Create clear, structured rubrics to grade each dimension of a task, and then grade each dimension with an isolated LLM-as-judge rather than using one to grade all dimensions"; "LLM-as-judge graders should be closely calibrated with human experts... given the subjective nature of research quality, LLM-based rubrics should be frequently calibrated against expert human judgment"; give the grader an escape hatch ("return 'Unknown' when it doesn't have enough information") to reduce hallucinated verdicts — "Demystifying evals for AI agents," Anthropic, https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents, lab engineering blog (primary, fetched directly). No specific numeric calibration threshold is published — the guidance is process-only.
- Protocol choices alone (handling of abstentions, choice of agreement metric, level of aggregation) can shift reported accuracy from 0.551 to 0.899 on the same underlying rubric-benchmark verdicts — a 34.8-point spread with zero actual judgment changes; the paper proposes an 11-item disclosure checklist (state the scale before picking a metric; report confusion matrices with N; report both human and judge abstention/"MET" rates; specify micro vs. macro vs. item-level aggregation) — "Agreement Measurement for Rubric-based LLM Judges: What to Report and Why," arXiv, https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00093, peer-reviewed/preprint methodology paper (primary, fetched directly).
- With atomic, self-contained rubric items, three independent LLM judge models showed substantial pairwise agreement and, when the same responses were re-graded across five independent runs, reached 99.2% unanimous judgement with average Cohen's κ of 0.983 — "Agentic Rubrics as Contextual Verifiers for SWE Agents," Scale AI, https://static.scale.com/uploads/654197dc94d34f66c0f5184e/Scale-Agentic-Rubrics.pdf, industry research paper (primary, via search summary).

**Evidence quality.** Primary throughout — one lab's own process guidance (Anthropic, prescriptive/qualitative, no numbers), one peer-reviewed methodology critique (quantified, directly relevant as a warning), and one industry benchmark paper (quantified, shows an achievable upper bound under good conditions). The three sources are complementary rather than redundant: Anthropic says *how* to calibrate, the methodology paper says *why naive calibration claims are unreliable*, and the Scale AI paper shows *what good looks like* when rubric design is done well.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves P2 ("Rubrics are the product"). Pipeline rule: every rubric the factory uses to grade a plan, spec, or review packet must (a) be decomposed into single-dimension judge calls, never one omnibus judge; (b) carry a documented calibration run against human graders on a labeled sample before being trusted in the pipeline, with the measurement protocol (abstention handling, aggregation level, metric choice) fixed and disclosed alongside the resulting agreement number, per the 11-item checklist; and (c) include an explicit "insufficient information" output option rather than forcing a pass/fail verdict.

**Gaps.** Did not find SWE-bench's own published rubric-calibration methodology directly (the SWE-bench-adjacent source found was a third-party Scale AI paper on "agentic rubrics," not the SWE-bench maintainers' own calibration writeup) — this is a partial miss against the question's named starting source.

### RQ-X-6. What engineering productivity metrics have organisations found misleading, and which survived?

**Answer.** Lines of code and any single productivity number used for individual performance review are the most consistently rejected metrics — Google's own engineering-practices book cites Dijkstra's framing that code should be counted as "lines spent," not "lines produced," and warns explicitly that using a productivity metric for individual performance reviews causes engineers to game it, which destroys its validity for organisational insight. What survived is not a single metric but a *refusal* to use one: Google's Goals/Signals/Metrics framework (tracking multiple dimensions together so improving one can't silently mask regression on another) and the SPACE framework (Satisfaction, Performance, Activity, Communication, Efficiency) from Microsoft Research/GitHub, whose explicit founding premise is that "developer productivity cannot be measured by a single metric or dimension."

**Patterns found.**
- Google explicitly warns against lines-of-code as a productivity metric, citing Dijkstra, and states that using any productivity metric for individual performance review causes gaming, invalidating it for org-level use; the recommended alternative is the Goals/Signals/Metrics (GSM) framework, deliberately tracking multiple dimensions together (referred to in the book as covering quality, engineer attention/interruptions, intellectual complexity, tempo/velocity, and satisfaction) to prevent trading one dimension off against another — *Software Engineering at Google*, ch. 7 ("Measuring Engineering Productivity"), https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch07.html, practitioner book published by Google engineers (primary, fetched directly).
- "Developer productivity cannot be measured by a single metric or dimension" — the SPACE framework's five dimensions (Satisfaction and well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication and collaboration, Efficiency and flow) were proposed specifically because teams that reduce productivity to one number make decisions on incomplete information — "The SPACE of Developer Productivity: There's more to it than you think," Forsgren, Storey, Maddila, Zimmermann, Houck & Butler, ACM Queue, 2021, published by Microsoft Research/GitHub researchers, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-space-of-developer-productivity-theres-more-to-it-than-you-think/, peer-reviewed-adjacent industry paper (primary source identified; full text behind an access wall in this pass, so quoted via the Microsoft Research abstract/summary rather than the full ACM Queue article).
- DORA's four keys (deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore) are widely adopted but reported by practitioners as blind to platform/infrastructure/refactoring work — such work "might not affect DORA metrics whatsoever" even when it substantially improves developer experience — and as gameable by inflating deployment frequency with trivial deploys — cited via secondary practitioner commentary (e.g., "The Promise and Peril of DORA Metrics," CodeAnt, https://www.codeant.ai/blogs/the-promise-and-peril-of-dora-metrics, and related secondary blogs), not the primary DORA/Accelerate report.

**Evidence quality.** The Google book source is primary and was read directly. The SPACE framework's core claim ("no single metric") is well-attested across multiple independent secondary summaries and the paper's own abstract, but the full ACM Queue text was not independently read in this pass (paywalled). The DORA-limitations claim is secondary-only — no primary DORA/Google Cloud "Accelerate" report text was fetched to confirm it, so it should be treated as practitioner consensus rather than an official DORA position.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly serves the charter's success measures (section 8). Pipeline rule: the factory should report its own success measures (revisions/ticket, questions/ticket, human minutes/ticket, share of tests kept, incidents) as an unranked panel, never collapsed into one composite score, and should never feed any single one of them into an individual engineer's performance evaluation — doing so would predictably produce the same gaming problem Google and SPACE both document for adjacent metrics.

**Gaps.** Did not fetch the primary DORA "Accelerate: State of DevOps" report text to independently confirm the platform-work-blindness and gaming claims — relied on secondary practitioner blogs. Did not fetch the full SPACE paper (ACM Queue, paywalled) — relied on the Microsoft Research abstract page and consistent secondary summaries.

### RQ-X-7. What do organisations and AI-coding teams say about keeping decisions and agent memory traceable and fresh: ADRs, decision logs, instruction files; what rots and what stays useful?

**Answer.** For standing agent instruction files (CLAUDE.md and equivalents), Anthropic's own official guidance and multiple independent practitioner reports converge on the same failure mode and the same fix: files rot silently — a line that stops being true gives no signal, the agent just starts behaving slightly wrong and the human blames the model — and the fix is a strict inclusion test ("would removing this line cause the agent to make mistakes? If not, cut it") applied on a recurring cadence, since an over-long file causes the agent to ignore instructions wholesale, including the ones that still matter. For decision records specifically, the ADR community's answer to staleness is structural rather than procedural: never edit an accepted record in place; supersede it with a new record and link the two, which keeps the log honest at the cost of requiring someone to actually notice and write the superseding record.

**Patterns found.**
- CLAUDE.md should hold only what changes agent behaviour and cannot be inferred from the code itself — bash commands the agent can't guess, non-default code-style rules, testing instructions, repo etiquette, project-specific architectural decisions, environment quirks, and non-obvious gotchas — and should explicitly exclude anything that changes frequently, restates self-evident practice, or duplicates what the agent can read from the code; the litmus test is "would removing this cause Claude to make mistakes? If not, cut it. Bloated CLAUDE.md files cause Claude to ignore your actual instructions"; the guide names "the over-specified CLAUDE.md" as one of a short list of common failure patterns, with the fix being to "ruthlessly prune" — "Best practices for Claude Code," Anthropic, https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices, official lab tool documentation (primary, fetched and read directly).
- Independent practitioner write-ups converge on the same diagnosis using different language: "instruction files rot silently — nothing alerts you when a line stops being true, so the agent just starts acting slightly wrong and you blame the model"; recommended practice is a recurring (e.g., monthly, ~15-minute) audit pass asking, per line, "is this still true?" and "has the agent actually violated this recently?", with stale rules removed rather than left in place, on the reasoning that "stale facts are worse than missing facts — they're confidently wrong" — multiple independent dev.to/blog write-ups (e.g., "How do you keep your AI agent skills from rotting silently?", https://dev.to/klymentiev/how-do-you-keep-your-ai-agent-skills-from-rotting-silently-2c5g; "How to Audit a Bloated CLAUDE.md File in 7 Steps," https://getunblocked.com/blog/audit-fix-bloated-claude-md/), practitioner reports (secondary — not lab-official, but multiple independent authors reach the same conclusion as Anthropic's own doc, which is a meaningful convergence).
- Accepted ADRs should never be edited in place; if a decision changes, write a new ADR that supersedes the old one and link them (status field: proposed / accepted / superseded, with a pointer to the superseding record), preserving a timeline of *why* direction shifted rather than silently rewriting history; linking each ADR to the pull request that implemented it is recommended specifically to close the gap between "we decided X" and "X is actually how the system works now," named as one of the main ways ADRs otherwise go stale — "Architectural Decision Records (ADRs)," https://adr.github.io/, and related secondary commentary, practitioner/community documentation of Michael Nygard's original ADR pattern (primary pattern source; some supporting detail via secondary blog commentary).

**Evidence quality.** The Anthropic source is primary, official, and was read in full. It is independently corroborated by several unrelated practitioner authors describing the same failure mode in their own words, which is a reasonably strong convergence for a qualitative (non-measured) claim. The ADR-supersession pattern is a long-standing, widely repeated community norm (originating with Nygard) rather than a single company's official policy, and no source in this pass provided measured data (e.g., what fraction of ADRs actually get superseded vs. silently ignored) — all evidence here is prescriptive/anecdotal, not measured.

Pipeline rule 1: apply Anthropic's behaviour-change litmus test to every rubric and instruction file the factory stores — if a line's removal would not change agent output, delete it rather than let it accumulate.
