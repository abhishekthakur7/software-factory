### RQ-S2-1. Which requirements frameworks include an explicit completeness test for acceptance criteria, and what does that test check?

**Answer.** Several frameworks provide a genuine completeness test rather than just a template. EARS constrains free-text requirements into six clause patterns (ubiquitous, event-driven "When", state-driven "While", optional-feature "Where", unwanted-behaviour "If...Then", and complex combinations), forcing every requirement to declare its trigger, precondition, actor, and response explicitly. Kiro turns this into a mechanical test: it auto-formalizes EARS-style acceptance criteria into SMT-lib logic and runs an SMT solver plus semantic-entropy sampling across multiple LLM formalizations to flag ambiguity, contradiction, and input regions left unspecified. INVEST's "Testable" clause is the completeness-relevant item in an otherwise human-applied 6-item checklist (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable): a story fails if no one can state a pass/fail test for it. Specification by Example and Given/When/Then make completeness observable rather than checkable by a machine — a requirement counts as complete when concrete, illustrative examples exist and run as executable "living documentation"; Cucumber's own docs operationalize this partly as a declarative-vs-imperative style test.

**Patterns found.**
- EARS's six-pattern syntax (ubiquitous / event-driven / state-driven / optional-feature / unwanted-behaviour / complex) constrains natural-language requirements into checkable clauses — Mavin, Wilkinson, Harwood, Novak, "Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (EARS)", IEEE RE'09 (2009), mirrored at https://alistairmavin.com/ears/, peer-reviewed conference paper (primary). No quantified defect-reduction data in the paper itself — it is a case study/experience report from Rolls-Royce aero-engine control requirements, not a controlled trial.
- Kiro's Requirements Analysis formalizes EARS acceptance criteria into SMT-lib (schemas, assertions, background constraints) and runs an SMT solver plus multi-sample "semantic entropy" measurement to detect wrong-level-of-detail, ambiguity, inconsistency, and incompleteness — Kiro (AWS), "Requirements analysis: catching requirement bugs before they become code", https://kiro.dev/blog/deep-spec-analysis/, company product documentation (primary). Cites external research (not independently verified here) that prompt ambiguity causes "Pass@1 drops of 20–40%, with 60–90% of syntactically valid code being semantically wrong."
- INVEST's "Testable" attribute is the operational completeness check for a story — Agile Alliance, "What does INVEST Stand For?", https://agilealliance.org/glossary/invest/, glossary/secondary reference to Bill Wake's 2003 coinage (primary essay not located, see Gaps).
- Specification by Example's "key examples" pattern: completeness is demonstrated through worked examples that double as automated tests ("living documentation") — Gojko Adzic, *Specification by Example* (Manning, 2011), https://www.manning.com/books/specification-by-example, practitioner book (publisher/summary page used; full text not fetched — secondary for this pass).
- Cucumber's official "declarative, not imperative" rule as a scenario-quality check: ask "will this wording need to change if the implementation does?" — Cucumber Docs, "Writing better Gherkin", https://cucumber.io/docs/bdd/better-gherkin/, official tool documentation (primary).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for the EARS paper, Kiro's own documentation, and Cucumber's official docs. Secondary for INVEST (glossary paraphrase, not Wake's original essay) and for Specification by Example (publisher/summary pages, not the book text). Multiple independent sources converge on the same structural point: EARS plus a formal layer (Kiro's SMT solver) is the only genuinely machine-checkable completeness test found; INVEST and Given/When/Then remain human-applied discipline unless a tool formalizes them, which is exactly what Kiro does.

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies directly. Rubric line: "Every acceptance criterion must be expressible in EARS form (precondition + trigger + system + response) and pass an automated formalization check; the S2 gate fails if formalization surfaces ambiguity (high variance across independent LLM formalizations), a contradiction, or an input region with no specified behaviour." Pipeline rule: apply INVEST's "Testable" and "Independent" checks as a secondary human-readable gate, and require at least one concrete Given/When/Then example per criterion before the ticket advances to S3.

**Gaps.** No controlled, denominator-bearing study was found showing EARS reduces defects (the original paper is a single-project case study, not an experiment). The primary Bill Wake essay on INVEST was searched for directly and not found among available sources — only secondary paraphrases (Agile Alliance, blogs) were located. Full text of Adzic's *Specification by Example* was not accessible (commercial book); the patterns reported here rely on publisher and Agile Alliance descriptions rather than the primary text.

### RQ-S2-2. What scenario categories do mature requirements or design practices force out of a requirement, and are there published checklists?

**Answer.** Google's SRE launch checklist and AWS's Well-Architected Framework both operationalize "forced categories" as fixed, service-agnostic checklists walked by a reviewer regardless of what the requirement author thought to mention, rather than leaving discovery to the author's judgment — but both are pre-launch/production-readiness gates, not requirements-stage-native tools. Google's checklist forces ten categories (architecture, machines/datacenters, capacity, reliability/failover, monitoring, security, automation including rollback and canary, growth, external-dependency degradation, and rollout scheduling) into every launch review. AWS's Operational Excellence pillar forces eleven named questions (OPS1–OPS11), including explicit ones on deployment-risk mitigation and workload-readiness. Neither source publishes a requirements-clarification-stage checklist by that name; both checklists are the closest published material and would need to be pulled forward to S2.

**Patterns found.**
- Ten-category Launch Coordination Checklist: architecture, machines/datacenters, volume/capacity/performance, system reliability and failover, monitoring, security, automation and manual tasks (incl. canary/staged rollout and change control), growth issues, external dependencies, schedule/rollout planning — Google, "Launch Checklist" (SRE book appendix), https://sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist/, company/lab practice doc, official mirror of the O'Reilly *Site Reliability Engineering* book (primary).
- AWS Well-Architected Framework, Operational Excellence pillar: 11 review questions (OPS1–OPS11), including OPS6 "How do you mitigate deployment risks?" and OPS7 "How do you know that you are ready to support a workload?" — AWS, Well-Architected Framework whitepaper, https://wa.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/2020-07-02T19-33-23/wat.pillar.operationalExcellence.en.html, official lab/vendor technical whitepaper (primary, non-marketing).
- Google design docs require a "cross-cutting concerns" section (security, privacy, observability named as examples) but explicitly leave the category list to be locally standardized rather than shipping a fixed checklist — Malte Ubl, "Design Docs at Google", https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/, practitioner/company account, primary author of the practice (primary).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for both the Google SRE checklist and the AWS whitepaper — both are official, non-marketing technical references. Two independent organisations converge on the same underlying mechanism (a standing checklist applied uniformly rather than left to author discretion), which strengthens the pattern, though neither is scoped to requirements-writing specifically. Uber's and Stripe's PRR checklists were searched for directly and not found publicly documented — only generic third-party (Cortex, OpsLevel, Port.io) PRR guidance surfaced, which was excluded as non-primary/vendor content per the sourcing rules.

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies with adaptation. Rubric line: "The S2 completeness check runs a fixed, versioned checklist of forced categories (error paths, concurrency, migration, backward compatibility, permissions, observability, rollback, data retention) against the acceptance criteria; any category with no matching criterion becomes a ranked question, not a silent gap." Pipeline rule: since no requirements-stage-native checklist was found, adapt Google's launch-checklist categories and AWS's OPS6/OPS7 questions downward into the S2 rubric rather than inventing one from scratch.

**Gaps.** No primary, publicly documented Uber or Stripe production-readiness/launch checklist was found (searched their engineering blogs directly; only third-party vendor summaries surfaced). No checklist was found published specifically for the requirements-clarification stage as opposed to pre-launch — this is a genuine gap in the literature that the pipeline design will need to bridge itself. Neither Google's nor AWS's material names "data retention" as an explicit line item; it appears only implicitly under security/compliance.

### RQ-S2-3. How do product and engineering practices decide which open questions must go to the product owner and which should be decided by a default assumption?

**Answer.** Three independent practices converge on the same underlying rule, stated in different vocabularies: an issue escalates to a decision-maker only when it is both consequential and hard to reverse; everything else is decided locally by whoever is closest to the work, who then discloses the decision rather than seeking prior approval. Shape Up puts this at shaping time — the shaper pre-resolves "rabbit holes" (details that could sink a fixed-appetite bet) and states "no-gos" explicitly, while leaving execution-level choices to the building team. Amazon's PR/FAQ pushes the same judgment earlier: the author must pre-answer every foreseeable internal-stakeholder question (finance, legal, ops, support) before the document is approved, so only genuinely unresolved, decision-relevant questions survive past approval. Apache's "lazy consensus" formalizes default-and-disclose for open-source governance explicitly around reversibility: no prior approval is required to act, only a public statement detailed enough to evaluate, with the actor prepared to reverse course if someone objects within a stated window.

**Patterns found.**
- Shape Up: the shaper resolves rabbit holes and states no-gos before a pitch is bet on; a complete pitch has five required ingredients — Problem, Appetite, Solution, Rabbit Holes, No-Gos — Ryan Singer, *Shape Up* ("Write the Pitch" and related chapters), https://basecamp.com/shapeup, practitioner book, freely published in full by Basecamp (primary).
- Amazon PR/FAQ: the Internal FAQ section must anticipate and pre-answer the hardest cross-functional questions (finance, legal, marketing, ops, customer support) before the document proceeds — Colin Bryar & Bill Carr, *Working Backwards*, summarized at https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/, practitioner book by former Amazon executives (primary account of the practice, accessed via secondary summary page — full book text not fetched).
- Apache lazy consensus: no explicit approval is required to proceed; state the proposal with enough detail for evaluation and be prepared to reverse it — "objecting too far down the road will cause upset, while objecting... early is likely to be greeted with relief that someone is watching and cares" — Apache Software Foundation, "Lazy Consensus", https://community.apache.org/committers/lazyConsensus.html, official governance documentation (primary).
- Assumption logs formalize the same decide-locally-and-disclose pattern with tracking: each assumption gets an ID, owner, priority (driven by uncertainty × impact), status, and closure date — ProjectManagement.com, "Assumptions Log", https://www.projectmanagement.com/deliverables/283302/assumptions-log, secondary practitioner/PMBOK-aligned material (not the primary PMBOK text itself).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for Shape Up (full text openly published) and Apache lazy consensus (official ASF documentation). Working Backwards evidence is secondary (summary site, not the book itself, though the summarized practice is from primary Amazon practitioners). Assumption-log criteria come from secondary standard-practice material. Three independent domains — product shaping, enterprise product review, and open-source governance — converge on the same reversibility/consequence-based split, which is meaningful cross-domain agreement even though no single source states it as a formula.

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies directly and maps onto the charter's existing S2 gate ("ranked question set, each with a proposed default... Answers, or accepts defaults"). Rubric line: "A question escalates to a human only if the answer is both consequence-bearing (materially changes the spec or plan) and not cheaply reversible after a default is accepted (P1, P4); otherwise the agent states a default and proceeds, recorded as an assumption." Pipeline rule: implement a per-ticket assumption log as a first-class artefact (owner = agent, status = accepted-default / confirmed / overridden) so accepted defaults stay visible and reversible, mirroring both lazy consensus's objection window and Shape Up's rabbit-hole resolution.

**Gaps.** No primary source was found using the exact term "decide-and-disclose" as named in the question's starting sources — a search for that phrase returned only an unrelated "Decide, Disclose, Document, Do" real-estate agency framework, which was excluded as irrelevant. No quantitative data was found on what share of Amazon's Internal-FAQ questions get answered by default assumption versus genuinely escalated — Amazon does not publish this breakdown.

### RQ-S2-4. What is known about clarifying-question quality for LLM agents: when to ask versus assume, how to rank questions, and how to avoid low-value questions?

**Answer.** Published research consistently documents a "recognition-action gap": LLM agents can identify that an instruction is ambiguous when explicitly asked to judge it, but by default they proceed silently rather than asking, and prompting or training them to interact measurably improves outcomes on underspecified tasks. The frontier of the research moves past a binary ask/don't-ask decision toward ranking candidate questions by expected information gain and stopping once further questions would not change the answer — using Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI) or semantic-entropy thresholds. Lab guidance (Anthropic, OpenAI) and product implementation (Kiro) converge on the same operational pattern independently: do not ask by default, but explicitly invoke an interview-style mode for underspecified or multi-file tasks, and skip questions entirely when the change is describable in one sentence.

**Patterns found.**
- Models recognize ambiguity when explicitly asked to judge it, yet in a QA setting they overwhelmingly default to direct answers; retrieved context widens this gap further by improving apparent answerability while making models less likely to ask — "Knowing but Not Showing: LLMs Recognize Ambiguity but Rarely Ask Clarifying Questions", https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25284, arXiv preprint (primary research; peer-review venue not confirmed).
- Without explicit prompting, models almost never interact even for severely underspecified inputs; prompting them to interact is reported to improve performance by up to 74% on underspecified tasks (figure attributed to an ICLR 2026 finding, sourced here via a secondary quote in a GitHub issue rather than the primary paper) — quoted in OpenAI/codex issue #11266, https://github.com/openai/codex/issues/11266, secondary (GitHub issue). Substantively corroborated by a separate, independently primary source: "Learning to Ask: When LLM Agents Meet Unclear Instruction" proposes Ask-when-Needed (AwN) and a NoisyToolBench benchmark for measuring this gap — https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00557, arXiv preprint (primary).
- "Structured Uncertainty guided Clarification for LLM Agents" ranks candidate clarifying questions by Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI) to decide which single question to ask next and when to stop asking — https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08798, arXiv preprint (primary).
- An uncertainty-aware multi-agent scaffold that separates "detect underspecification" from "execute the task" reaches a 69.40% task-resolve rate on an underspecified variant of SWE-bench Verified, conserving queries on simple tasks and asking proactively on complex ones — "Ask or Assume? Uncertainty-Aware Clarification-Seeking in Coding Agents", https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26233, arXiv preprint (primary; denominator = underspecified SWE-bench Verified task set).
- Training with preference labels that simulate future-turn outcomes (rather than judging the clarifying question in isolation) improves F1 by roughly 5% on multi-interpretation open-domain QA and improves the accuracy of the ask-vs-answer decision by roughly 3% — "Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions", https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13788, arXiv preprint (primary; percentages reported without full benchmark-size detail — treat as approximate).
- Anthropic's official guidance: use plan mode's explore-then-plan workflow for ambiguous or multi-file tasks and skip it when the change is describable in one sentence; a documented "let Claude interview you" pattern explicitly invokes the AskUserQuestion tool with the instruction to "not ask obvious questions, dig into the hard parts... until we've covered everything," then write a spec file before implementation starts — Anthropic, "Best practices for Claude Code", https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices, official lab documentation (primary).
- OpenAI Codex: clarifying-question behaviour is present in Plan Mode but largely absent by default in normal mode; users are told to explicitly request a "reverse interview" ("ask Codex to question you first... challenge your assumptions") for fuzzy ideas — OpenAI, Codex best-practices docs, https://developers.openai.com/codex/learn/best-practices, official lab documentation (primary), corroborated by an open community issue asking to extend clarifying-question behaviour beyond Plan Mode, https://github.com/openai/codex/issues/11266 (primary issue-tracker evidence of the current gap).
- Kiro's Requirements Analysis operationalizes "when to ask": it measures semantic entropy across multiple independent LLM formalizations of a requirement, and high entropy (multiple plausible logical formalizations) is the trigger for a clarifying question, each posed as a two-option question naming the affected requirement, a plain-language explanation, and a suggested default — Kiro, https://kiro.dev/blog/deep-spec-analysis/, company product documentation (primary).

**Evidence quality.** Mostly primary — arXiv preprints (research literature, peer-review status not independently confirmed for any of them) plus official lab documentation from Anthropic and OpenAI, which is as primary as available for "what the labs say." Measured figures with denominators exist for two papers (69.40% task-resolve rate on underspecified SWE-bench Verified; ~5% F1 / ~3% accuracy gains on open-domain QA benchmarks), but the benchmarks differ in kind (coding agent vs. open-domain QA) and should not be pooled. At least four independent research groups (2409.00557, 2410.13788, 2511.08798, 2605.25284, plus 2603.26233) converge on the same qualitative finding — default LLM behaviour under-asks relative to actual ambiguity — which is a robust cross-source result even though the underlying literature is recent (2024–2026) preprints rather than settled peer-reviewed consensus.

**Transfer to an agent.** Directly on-target for FM-07 and the charter's S2 exit criterion. Rubric line: "A candidate question is surfaced to the human only if its expected value of information (impact on the spec or plan × probability the stated default is wrong) exceeds a defined threshold; below it, the agent states the default and logs it as an assumption per P4." Pipeline rule: implement the EVPI/semantic-entropy pattern operationally the way Kiro does — generate several independent formalizations of each acceptance criterion, treat high variance across them as the ambiguity signal, and rank surfaced questions by that variance, which directly serves the charter's "ranked question set" and "batching and ranking" requirements (section 6).

**Gaps.** No published measured data was found on user response to question *volume* specifically — e.g., a study showing engineers stop reading past N questions in a coding-agent context. The charter's FM-07 cost claim ("humans stop reading questions") is plausible given general survey-fatigue literature, but no coding-agent-specific measurement of this was located. The "up to 74%" improvement figure traces to a secondary quote (a GitHub issue citing an "ICLR 2026" result) rather than a primary paper located directly in this search pass; treat it as unverified pending the original source. No data was found from Anthropic or OpenAI on measured acceptance/override rates for AskUserQuestion-generated defaults in production use — only qualitative usage guidance exists publicly.

### RQ-S2-5. How do spec-driven development tools structure requirements, design, and tasks, and what failure modes do their users report?

**Answer.** The three actively documented spec-driven-development tools converge on the same three-artefact chain — requirements/spec, design/plan, tasks — with an explicit, mostly optional formalization gate between requirements and design, but practitioner commentary describes the same two failure modes recurring across all of them: specs silently drifting from the implementation once editing continues outside the tool's own flow, and structured-looking specs producing false confidence because they carry the *appearance* of rigor (EARS formatting, acceptance criteria) without the substance, which gets rubber-stamped in review. Kiro's chain is requirements.md (EARS notation) → design.md → tasks.md, with an optional but recommended SMT-based "Analyze Requirements" gate. GitHub spec-kit's chain is constitution → specify → (clarify, optional) → plan → tasks → (analyze, optional) → implement → converge. OpenSpec deliberately narrows scope to per-change deltas (proposal.md → specs/ delta files → design.md → tasks.md → archive) specifically to avoid maintaining one large, drifting spec, using ADDED/MODIFIED/REMOVED notation instead of a monolithic document.

**Patterns found.**
- Kiro's artefact chain (requirements.md/EARS → design.md → tasks.md), with an optional deep SMT-solver-based "Analyze Requirements" gate that surfaces ambiguity/contradiction/gap findings as clarifying questions before design begins — Kiro (AWS) docs, https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/feature-specs/ and https://kiro.dev/blog/deep-spec-analysis/, company product documentation (primary).
- GitHub spec-kit's artefact chain (constitution → specify → clarify [optional] → plan → tasks → analyze [optional] → implement → converge), with clarify recommended specifically to address underspecified areas before planning — GitHub, spec-kit README, https://github.com/github/spec-kit, official tool documentation (primary).
- OpenSpec's change-scoped chain (proposal.md → specs/ delta files with ADDED/MODIFIED/REMOVED tags → design.md → tasks.md → archive), explicitly built so specs describe one change at a time rather than accumulating into a single drifting document — OpenSpec (Fission-AI) README, https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec, official tool documentation (primary).
- Reported failure mode — spec drift: an unnamed practitioner describes abandoning spec synchronization because "it just keeps drifting and drifting until you have duplication and contradictions across specs… maintaining the main specs is not worth it" — quoted via codemyspec.com, secondary aggregator of a Hacker News discussion, anecdotal, single source.
- Reported failure mode — rubber-stamping from false rigor: "Kiro's requirements and design documents are only as good as the initial description you give it. A vague prompt produces a vague spec, but now it's a vague spec with the appearance of rigor... That's worse than no spec at all" — architecturecorner.substack.com, "Specification-Driven Development", secondary/practitioner commentary, anecdotal (single author's argument, not a measured study).
- Same source names the mitigation as "cultural, not technical": treat spec review as a real design review rather than a formality — architecturecorner.substack.com, secondary, anecdotal.

**Evidence quality.** Primary for the three tools' documented artefact chains (official docs/READMEs). The reported failure modes (drift, rubber-stamping) are secondary and anecdotal — single-author blog posts and a forum quote, not measured studies with denominators — though the same two failure modes recur across independent commentators, which raises confidence the pattern is real without making it quantified evidence.

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies directly. Rubric line: "The S2→S3 gate requires a machine-checkable formalization pass (contradiction/ambiguity/completeness check) before a spec is presentable to a human, so a syntactically well-formed but semantically vague spec cannot pass on appearance alone." Pipeline rule: scope specs to the change under work (OpenSpec's delta pattern) rather than maintaining one evergreen document, and re-run the completeness check whenever the plan or implementation diverges from the approved requirements — directly countering the drift failure mode all three tools report or design around.

**Gaps.** No measured data (e.g., percentage of specs that drifted, or time-to-drift, or rubber-stamp rate) was found for any of the three tools — all drift/rubber-stamp evidence located is anecdotal. Tessl, named in the starting sources, yielded only a registry/product page rather than practitioner failure-mode reports; a separate search for Tessl-specific practitioner accounts returned nothing independent.

### RQ-S2-6. How do teams split large product requirements, and how do they detect that a ticket is too large to specify safely?

**Answer.** Story-splitting practice (Richard Lawrence) and Shape Up both treat "fails to deliver a distinguishable increment of value" as the underlying test for whether a requirement is too large to specify safely, and Lawrence's nine named patterns (workflow steps, CRUD operations, business-rule variations, data variations, data-entry method, major-effort deferral, simple/complex split, defer-performance, spike) give a concrete cutting method that keeps each resulting slice vertical and INVEST-compliant. Shape Up instead bounds size upfront rather than splitting after the fact: a fixed time "appetite" forces scope down to fit, rather than scope determining time. Google's published guidance on code-change-list (CL) size is the closest measured link between size and rework/review cost, though it measures code diffs rather than requirement size directly: small CLs (Google's own guidance: roughly 100 lines is reasonable, roughly 1,000 lines is usually too large) are reviewed faster and are less likely to contain bugs, because both author and reviewer can reason about the CL's full effect.

**Patterns found.**
- Nine canonical story-splitting patterns: workflow steps, operations (CRUD), business rule variations, data variations, data entry methods, major effort, simple/complex, defer performance, spike — Richard Lawrence, "The Humanizing Work Guide to Splitting User Stories", https://www.humanizingwork.com/the-humanizing-work-guide-to-splitting-user-stories/, practitioner practice doc, original author (primary).
- Too-large/unsplittable signal: a story that fails INVEST's "Valuable" test (no observable increment of user value) cannot be properly split — "If you start with something that isn't an increment of value, there's no way to slice it smaller and get an increment of value" — same source (primary).
- Shape Up bounds requirement size upfront through a fixed time "appetite" (e.g. six weeks) rather than splitting an oversized story after the fact — fixed time, variable scope — Ryan Singer, *Shape Up*, https://basecamp.com/shapeup, practitioner book (primary).
- Google's CL-size guidance: roughly 100 lines is a reasonable CL size, roughly 1,000 lines is usually too large; small CLs are less likely to introduce bugs because author and reviewer can reason about the CL's full effect — Google, "Small CLs", eng-practices repository, https://github.com/google/eng-practices/blob/master/review/developer/small-cls.md, company engineering practice documentation (primary).
- Patch/change size negatively affects code-review effectiveness broadly, and review time increases with patch size — "An Industrial Case Study on Shrinking Code Review Changesets through Remark Prediction", https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09510, industrial case-study paper (primary), with Chromium's contributor guide independently stating "review time often increases exponentially with patch size" (secondary corroboration).

**Evidence quality.** Primary for the story-splitting patterns (Lawrence's own site), Shape Up (Singer's own book), and Google's CL-size guidance (official eng-practices repository), with an independent academic industrial case study corroborating the size-versus-review-effectiveness relationship. This is one of the better-evidenced answers in this packet: three independent lines of evidence (practitioner story-splitting literature, Shape Up, and Google's empirically grounded CL guidance plus an academic study) converge, though the quantitative evidence measures code-diff size, not requirement/ticket size — no single source measures the full chain from "large requirement" through "large diff" to "more rework."

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies directly. Rubric line: "At S2, if a requirement cannot be described as a single vertical slice with observable value (fails INVEST's Valuable test), or its estimated diff exceeds the team's small-CL threshold (~100 lines / fewer than ~10 files), the agent must propose a split using the workflow-step / CRUD / business-rule / data-variation patterns before requirements are marked complete, citing FM-05 and FM-06." Pipeline rule: treat estimated ticket size (files touched, services touched, count of unresolved unknowns) as a first-class S2 signal feeding the ticket-complexity classification the charter leaves open at Q3, using Google's CL-size bands as a starting threshold.

**Gaps.** No source directly measures rework or defect rate as a function of requirement/story size, as distinct from code-diff size — the closest available evidence (Google's CL-size-to-review-outcome data) is a proxy, not a direct measurement of "requirement too large." No published data was found quantifying how often applying Lawrence's splitting patterns reduces rework in practice — the patterns are prescriptive and practitioner-endorsed, not evaluated in a controlled study.

### RQ-S2-7. How do organisations record which requirement questions were asked, answered, or defaulted, so that the record survives to review and rollout?

**Answer.** The recurring mechanism across Amazon, ADR practice, and (by contrast) Google is a durable, named artefact — a FAQ, a status-plus-alternatives record, or an assumption log — that persists inside or alongside the requirements document rather than in ephemeral chat or ticket comments, built to be read by someone who was not present when the question was raised. Amazon's PR/FAQ makes this the primary artefact: the Internal FAQ exists specifically to pre-answer stakeholder questions across functions (finance, legal, ops, support) and survive into later review. ADR practice uses a status field (proposed/accepted/rejected/deprecated/superseded) and, in the community-extended MADR template, an explicit "Alternatives Considered" section, so a later reader sees not just the decision but the rejected options and why. Notably, the publicly documented Google design-doc template has no dedicated open-questions or decision-log section — unresolved issues get folded into ongoing edits to "Alternatives considered," which is a real gap relative to Amazon's and ADR's more explicit, purpose-built mechanisms.

**Patterns found.**
- Amazon PR/FAQ's Internal FAQ pre-answers and permanently records the hard cross-functional questions (finance, legal, marketing, ops, customer support) that stakeholders would raise, so the record survives into later review and rollout — Colin Bryar & Bill Carr, *Working Backwards*, summarized at https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/, practitioner book by former Amazon executives (primary account, via secondary summary page — full book text not fetched).
- ADR status field (proposed / accepted / rejected / deprecated / superseded-by) plus, in the MADR community extension, an explicit "Alternatives Considered" section for options evaluated and rejected — Michael Nygard's original ADR format and the MADR extension, https://adr.github.io/adr-templates/, official ADR community documentation (primary for template structure).
- Google design docs fold decisions and trade-offs into "Alternatives considered," with no separately named open-questions or decision-log section documented in the publicly available template; unresolved issues are handled by editing the doc as work proceeds rather than by a discrete tracked log — Malte Ubl, "Design Docs at Google", https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/, practitioner/company account (primary).
- Assumption logs (project-management practice, PMI-aligned) formalize question-to-decision traceability as a standalone artefact: ID, description, owner, status, closure date — ProjectManagement.com, "Assumptions Log", https://www.projectmanagement.com/deliverables/283302/assumptions-log, secondary/standard-practice summary.

**Evidence quality.** Primary for ADR template structure (official ADR community docs) and for the Google design-doc account (Ubl's own post, describing the absence of a dedicated open-questions section). Amazon's PR/FAQ evidence comes through a secondary summary of the primary book. No source directly measures whether these records are actually consulted by later readers — this "good answer" criterion (evidence the record helps later readers) was searched for and not found in any source located.

**Transfer to an agent.** Applies directly. Rubric line: "Every accepted default and every answered clarifying question is written into a persistent per-ticket assumption/decision log (question, proposed default, answer or acceptance, who and when) that ships with the spec and carries forward unmodified into the S3 plan and the S6 review packet, per P4 and the charter's human interaction contract (section 6)." Pipeline rule: since Google's own published template lacks a dedicated open-questions section, do not imitate it here — follow Amazon's Internal-FAQ and ADR's status-plus-alternatives pattern instead, since both are purpose-built to survive to later readers.

**Gaps.** No published evidence — from Amazon, Google, or ADR literature — was found measuring whether these logs are actually reused by later readers (citation rates, time saved, or reduced re-litigation of settled questions). This mirrors a gap also relevant to RQ-S3-6 in this research agenda and appears to be a genuine hole in the public literature rather than a result of search-term choice — the same search terms surfaced abundant template guidance but no outcome measurement for any of the three record types.
